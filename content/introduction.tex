\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Urban Air Mobility (UAM) is emerging as a promising extension of future transportation systems. To enable the safe integration of large numbers of electric vertical take-off and landing (eVTOL) vehicles into low-altitude airspace, structured traffic-management frameworks such as the European U-space~\cite{eu_commission_2017,easa2020uspace} and the U.S. UTM concept~\cite{kopardekar2016utm} have been proposed. These initiatives emphasize service-oriented architectures and trajectory-based operations to ensure scalable and safe airspace integration~\cite{sesar_conops_2019,sesar_master_plan_2025}.

A central requirement in such environments is scalable real-time path planning. In dense urban scenarios, large numbers of trajectory requests must be processed while ensuring separation minima and compliance with structured corridor constraints. Search-based approaches such as A* have been widely investigated for UAV routing~\cite{sahawneh2016_3d_path_planning,a_star_2d3d_comparison_utm}, alongside 4D grid-based conflict management strategies~\cite{acevedo_4d_grids_u_space}. Deconfliction in multi-dimensional trajectory spaces has been studied in trajectory-based operations frameworks~\cite{NDMap,enea_porretta_4d_tbo_comparison}, and surveys summarize the range of available UAV planning methods~\cite{ahmad2023_uav_path_planning_survey}.

In operational U-space systems, however, path planning is tightly coupled with trajectory generation~\cite{morscheck2021cfms}, deconfliction~\cite{NDMap}, and verification services~\cite{sesar_conops_2019}. Monolithic implementations of these components may limit scalability and extensibility. Microservice architectures offer a modular alternative, enabling loosely coupled services with independent scalability~\cite{dragoni2017microservices,matlekovic2020uav}. In the architecture considered in this work, services communicate through RESTful HTTP interfaces and exchange structured data using JSON messages. This approach allows individual components---such as topology services, planning services, deconfliction services, and authorization interfaces---to be deployed and scaled independently while maintaining clear interface boundaries. Nevertheless, quantitative evaluations of integrated, microservice-based U-space planning systems remain limited, particularly with respect to latency decomposition and computational scaling with path distance.

This paper presents a microservice-oriented path-planning architecture and evaluates its baseline computational performance. The system separates (i) a global A*-based route planner and CFMS-based 4D trajectory-generation pipeline in the \texttt{acc-trajcalc} path-planning service from (ii) NDMap-based deconfliction in the \texttt{acc-trajdeconf} service~\cite{NDMap,morscheck2021cfms}. A CesiumJS-based frontend enables interactive visualization of time-dependent trajectories~\cite{cesiumjs_software}.

The central planning pipeline evaluated in this paper follows four stages. First, A* selects a shortest corridor route on the vertiport graph. Second, waypoint synthesis augments this route with terminal, climb, cruise, descent, and landing structure. Third, CFMS converts the structured waypoint sequence into a dense time-parameterized 4D trajectory. Finally, the runtime analysis decomposes these stages and shows that CFMS trajectory generation, rather than graph search, dominates planner-stage latency.

The contributions of this work are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item A service-oriented U-space planning architecture that separates topology provision, path planning, trajectory generation, and NDMap-based deconfliction into dedicated services.
    \item A CFMS-compatible waypoint-synthesis pipeline that converts A*-selected corridor routes into phase-structured inputs for 4D trajectory generation.
    \item A latency decomposition of the planner pipeline into A* path computation, waypoint synthesis, and CFMS trajectory generation.
    \item A Hamburg vertiport corridor-network evaluation using sequential trajectory requests to quantify planner-stage computational scaling.
\end{itemize}

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section~\ref{sec:related} reviews related work, Section~\ref{sec:methodology} describes the system design, Section~\ref{sec:experimental_setup} outlines the evaluation setup, and Section~\ref{sec:results} presents the results.
