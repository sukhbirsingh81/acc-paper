\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The results show that the evaluated service decomposition is well aligned with
the computational structure of the path-planning problem. A* contributes only a
small fraction of the planner-stage runtime, whereas trajectory generation
dominates the measured cost. This indicates that graph-based routing on the
evaluated corridor network is not the main computational bottleneck. Instead, the
limiting stage is the conversion of a selected corridor route into a
kinematically feasible 4D trajectory.

This is relevant for contingency operations. If an emergency or abnormal
condition requires a new route to a suitable vertiport, the low A* runtime
suggests that lateral route alternatives can be recomputed quickly after graph
changes such as corridor closures, geofence activation, or deconfliction-driven
edge penalties. The operational delay would mainly come from regenerating the
corresponding 4D trajectory, not from searching the corridor graph.

The main optimization opportunity is therefore trajectory generation. Long
routes often contain cruise segments between A* nodes where speed, heading, and
altitude remain nearly constant. Adaptive trajectory sampling could exploit
this structure by using lower resolution during straight cruise and higher
resolution near take-off, descent, turns, and landing. Such a strategy could
reduce processing cost and data volume while preserving the information needed
in dynamically changing or geometrically complex parts of the flight.

Further optimization is possible at the route and segment level. Common
origin--destination routes could be cached when the corridor graph and cruise
layer are unchanged, and frequently reused corridor segments could be
precomputed to avoid repeated trajectory-generation work. Another important
separation is between sampling for visualization and sampling for operational
uses such as conflict checking or trajectory-internal constraints. A coarse
visualization representation may be sufficient for display, whereas conflict
checking may require different temporal or spatial resolution near encounters,
turns, and terminal procedures.

The evaluation remains a baseline computational scaling study. Conflict
detection and resolution are disabled, requests are processed sequentially, and
the scenario uses a fixed cruise layer and static corridor graph. Thus, the
reported results should not be interpreted as throughput measurements of a
distributed operational deployment. Future work should evaluate concurrent
requests, larger graphs, closed-loop interaction with acc-trajdeconf, and the
trajectory-generation optimizations discussed above.
