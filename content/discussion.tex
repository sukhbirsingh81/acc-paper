\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The results show that the evaluated service decomposition is well aligned with
the computational structure of the path-planning problem. A* contributes only a
small fraction of the planner-stage runtime, whereas trajectory generation
dominates the measured cost. This indicates that graph-based routing on the
evaluated corridor network is not the main scalability bottleneck. Instead, the
limiting stage is the conversion of a selected corridor route into a physically
feasible 4D trajectory.

This is relevant for contingency operations. If an emergency or abnormal
condition requires a new route to a suitable vertiport, the low A* runtime
suggests that lateral route alternatives can be recomputed quickly after graph
changes such as corridor closures, geofence activation, or deconfliction-driven
edge penalties. The operational delay would mainly come from regenerating the
corresponding 4D trajectory, not from searching the corridor graph.

The main optimization opportunity is therefore trajectory generation. Long
routes often contain cruise segments between A* nodes where speed, heading, and
altitude remain nearly constant. Reducing the number of generated trajectory
points in such segments, while preserving higher resolution near take-off,
descent, turns, and landing, could reduce processing cost and data volume
without significantly reducing operational information.

The evaluation remains a baseline computational study. Conflict detection and
resolution are disabled, requests are processed sequentially, and the scenario
uses a fixed cruise layer and static corridor graph. Thus, the reported results
should not be interpreted as throughput measurements of a distributed
operational deployment. Future work should evaluate concurrent requests, larger
graphs, closed-loop interaction with acc-trajdeconf, and adaptive trajectory
sampling or caching to reduce the dominant trajectory-generation cost.
