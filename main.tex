\begin{abstract}
Urban air mobility requires scalable path-planning and conflict-management services for structured low-altitude operations. The European U-space framework provides such services within designated airspace volumes rather than prescribing corridors; nevertheless, corridor-based route structures are envisioned by several operators for early urban air mobility implementation. This paper presents a service-oriented four-dimensional path-planning framework for structured corridor operations within U-space that combines graph-based shortest-path routing, flight-management-based time-parameterized trajectory generation, and multi-dimensional conflict checking with request-local lateral re-routing. The architecture separates topology provision, route planning, waypoint synthesis, trajectory generation, deconfliction, and interaction with the U-space service provider into distinct service responsibilities. The prototype is evaluated on a Hamburg vertiport corridor network using two local, sequential experiments. First, a planner-stage scaling experiment with deconfliction disabled decomposes runtime into path search, waypoint synthesis, trajectory generation, and the planner-stage subtotal. Path search and waypoint synthesis remain sub-millisecond in the evaluated benchmark, while invocation of the trajectory generator dominates planner-stage runtime and determines the observed scaling with selected path distance. Second, a conflict-resolution-enabled experiment checks each candidate trajectory against previously accepted trajectories and applies lateral re-routing by request-local edge removal. In 100 sequential operator flight-plan requests, 97 requests were accepted: 93 were conflict-free on the first deconfliction check, one was the first request with no peer trajectory available, and three were successfully resolved by lateral re-routing. Three requests failed, consisting of one retry-limit case and two routing/input-graph errors. The results should be interpreted as a local, sequential baseline evaluation of a service-oriented prototype, not as an operational capacity assessment.
\end{abstract}
