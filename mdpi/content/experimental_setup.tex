\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:experimental_setup}

\subsection{Scenario and Network Model}
\label{sec:scenario_setup}

All experiments use an exemplary vertiport network in Hamburg. The vertiport locations come from the Hamburg UAM scenario of earlier DLR studies in the HorizonUAM project~\cite{abdellaoui2025_airspace_uam,schuchardt2023_atm_uam_review}. The corridor connections between the vertiports were defined by us for this study, so that the benchmark graph is connected. Each vertiport is a graph node placed at the average of its pad coordinates, and each corridor is an undirected edge. All corridors use one fixed cruise layer at \SI{200}{m} above mean sea level. This fixed-altitude network without obstacles is a benchmark simplification that makes the experiments repeatable.

Terrain and obstacles are therefore not modeled, and the corridor layer is given above MSL so that all experiments are comparable. The edge weights are the distances between the connected vertiports, computed with the Haversine formula. The routing cost is therefore only a two-dimensional distance: there are no extra costs for altitude changes, no-fly areas, turns, weather or other operational factors.

With this simplification, the shortest-path routing is repeatable and the geographic layout of the network is kept. Figure~\ref{fig:hamburg_corridor_network} shows the Hamburg vertiport corridor network used in the evaluation.

The shortest-path values are computed over all \(29 \times 28 = 812\) ordered origin--destination (OD) pairs of the graph, not only over the benchmark sample. The graph is small, so graph search is fast, but it is connected enough to give routes with several hops. Each undirected corridor is stored as two directed edges, one per direction. Every node is a vertiport; there are no separate corridor-intersection nodes, and a route can fly over a vertiport on the way without stopping there. The original vertiport dataset has 61 entries. Duplicate entries, entries without any corridor connection, and five airports that we left out of the benchmark reduce it to the 29 nodes used here. The node list and edge list are stored in the service repository and are available as described in the Data Availability Statement.

\subsection{System Configuration}
\label{sec:system_configuration}

All experiments ran on a laptop with an Intel Core i7-8650U central processing unit (CPU) at 1.90\,GHz with 4 cores and 8 threads, and 16\,GB of random-access memory (RAM), under a 64-bit operating system.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Operating system:} Ubuntu 24.04 long-term support (LTS) release (Noble Numbat), running under Windows Subsystem for Linux 2 (WSL2), as recorded in the experiment manifest.
    \item \textbf{CFMS version:} CFMS project version~1.0; the recorded planner revision pins its source dependency to commit \texttt{b6532f4}.
    \item \textbf{NDMap version:} Used inside the Deconfliction Service; no standalone release or execution-time dependency revision was recorded. The recovered dependency and the limits of its provenance are documented below.
\end{itemize}

The Planning Service and CFMS ran locally on the same machine. The Deconfliction Service was part of the same local deployment, but it is disabled in the planner benchmark below, so that only routing and trajectory generation are measured. We did not use a distributed deployment, containers, or artificial network delay. The runtimes are therefore a single-machine baseline and not the latency of a distributed system.

\paragraph{Software provenance.}
The preserved rerun notes identify Planning Service revision \texttt{92c10ab}. They describe a Deconfliction Service closest-approach correction whose committed counterpart is \texttt{2ee9143}. We preserved these sources, their dependency lockfiles, the planner's pinned CFMS dependency, and the recovered NDMap dependency (\texttt{6fc3a9c}) in an internal archive with checksums. The accompanying reproducibility record distinguishes source-history recovery from execution-time evidence: the original runs did not record a detector-binary checksum, the NDMap dependency revision, or the resolved aircraft-performance-file checksum. Consequently, the recovered archive supports reconstruction but does not establish exact identity of every component executed in the original runs.

The system is split into services, but the tested deployment is not distributed over several hosts. Scaling it out over several machines would need more work, for example a shared trajectory store, shared access to the conflict-detection data, and orchestration between the services.

\subsection{Planner, Generator, and Deconfliction Configuration}
\label{sec:planner_configuration}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Global planner:} The Planning Service performs A* search on the undirected corridor graph using Haversine distance both as edge cost and as admissible heuristic,
    \[
        f(n) = g(n) + h(n).
    \]
    The optimization objective is therefore purely 2D shortest-path distance between vertiports; altitude, no-fly constraints, and other operational cost terms are not part of the route cost model.

    \item \textbf{Deconfliction:} After CFMS has generated the 4D trajectory, the Deconfliction Service uses NDMap to check it against all trajectories that were already accepted. NDMap is not part of the Planning Service. The Deconfliction Service only detects and reports conflicts; the Planning Service changes the request-local graph and re-routes.

    \item \textbf{Cruise altitude:} One fixed cruise layer at \SI{200}{m} MSL, independent of pad elevation, terrain and obstacles. This is a benchmark assumption and not an operational airspace model.

    \item \textbf{Waypoint synthesis:} Before CFMS is called, the planner turns each corridor route into a short waypoint sequence, using the simplified verticone geometry of Section~\ref{sec:trajectory_generation}. For a route with several nodes, the nominal sequence is:
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item origin pad waypoint,
        \item vertical climb to \(h_1\),
        \item centerline transition waypoint at \(h_2\),
        \item optional outer-limitation-surface (OLS) waypoint up to \SI{152}{m} above the pad, capped by cruise altitude,
        \item intermediate corridor nodes with altitude assigned by the design climb gradient until the fixed cruise layer is reached,
        \item optional top-of-descent waypoint on the inbound centerline,
        \item OFV entry waypoint at \(h_2\),
        \item over-pad waypoint at \(h_1\),
        \item landing decision point (LDP), and
        \item destination pad waypoint.
    \end{enumerate}
    For a direct origin--destination route, the corridor nodes are replaced by one intermediate great-circle waypoint between departure and arrival. The terminal geometry is a rule-based construction inspired by the EASA vertiport procedures, while the en-route corridor graph is only used as a centerline reference and not as a validated corridor volume.

    \item \textbf{Trajectory generation:} CFMS generates the 4D trajectory using explicit phase-dependent speed targets and the selected aircraft-performance model. These targets are benchmark settings, not a uniform percentage reduction from aircraft limits (Table~\ref{tab:aircraft_configuration}).
\end{itemize}

The benchmark request builder does not specify an aircraft type. In the recorded planner source, the default selects a multicopter model and the file \nolinkurl{data/uam_performance_params.txt}; a service environment variable can override that file. The default file and its checksum are preserved, but the original service environment and resolved file path were not logged. Table~\ref{tab:aircraft_configuration} therefore distinguishes explicit settings in the planner source from reconstructed default model parameters. The default model describes a generic multirotor eVTOL aircraft of the UAM class, with 18 rotors and an empty mass of \SI{950}{kg}, and is used here as a representative vehicle for trajectory generation. The default file identifies electrical and battery parameters as uncalibrated. It is an illustrative benchmark configuration, not a validated representation of a particular aircraft, and the experiments do not assess energy consumption, endurance or aircraft certification.

\subsection{Evaluation Methodology}
\label{sec:evaluation_methodology}

\paragraph{Experiment A: planner-stage latency decomposition and route-length dependence.}
To measure the baseline computation time, we sent one batch of trajectory requests one after another to the Planning Service, with deconfliction disabled. Origin, destination and route geometry vary between requests; the network (29 nodes) and sequential request protocol stay fixed. Route length is an observed predictor, not an independently controlled variable. This experiment therefore breaks down the latency and shows how it depends on route length on one network. It is not a scalability or throughput test.

For this experiment, \paPairs{} origin--destination vertiport pairs were drawn without replacement from all possible directed pairs. The sample is defined by its recorded pair list and not by a seed: origin, destination, selected route, path distance, number of trajectory samples and all timings of every request are stored in the benchmark comma-separated values (CSV) file, so the recorded requests and analyses can be reconstructed. Exact timing reproduction also depends on the software and execution environment. Duplicate vertiports and vertiports without corridor connections were not part of the sample, which gives the 29-node network of Table~\ref{tab:network_stats}. Origin and destination were always different, and no pair appeared twice. Five warmup requests were sent first and not recorded. Each OD pair was then measured \paReps{} times, which gives \paObs{} timing observations. All requests succeeded, so nothing had to be removed from the results. Each request was completed before the next one started, so there were no effects from parallel processing. All times are measured with monotonic high-resolution clocks (\texttt{time.perf\_counter} on client and server). The laptop CPU of Section~\ref{sec:system_configuration} ran with the default frequency scaling of the operating system; we did not fix the CPU governor or turn off turbo boost, which adds some variance between runs.

\paragraph{Sample units in Experiment~A.}
The experiment has two different sample units, and we keep them apart. There are \paObs{} timing observations but only \paPairs{} different route geometries, because the \paReps{} repetitions of one OD pair use the same origin, destination, route, distance and number of samples; only the measured times change. The runtime distributions use all \paObs{} timing observations. Regressions, slope confidence intervals and Pearson correlations instead use one mean per OD pair, giving \paPairs{} observations for route-level analysis. Averaging equal numbers of repetitions leaves the fitted slopes unchanged because distance and sample count are fixed within each pair. It reduces within-pair timing noise but does not guarantee a particular change in confidence-interval width. We compute conventional ordinary-least-squares 95\% slope intervals with \(n-2\) degrees of freedom; these assume independent, constant-variance regression errors. Routes share network segments and measurements share one execution session, so the intervals are descriptive, model-based summaries of this benchmark rather than uncertainty bounds for other networks or deployments.

Quantiles are taken directly from the sorted sample without interpolation: \(p_{90}\) is the value at zero-based index \(\lfloor 0.90\,(n-1) \rfloor\), and \(p_{50}\) is the normal median (the mean of the two middle values for even \(n\)). The counts \(n\) shown in the distance-bucket figures are timing observations.

We recorded the runtime separately for:
\begin{itemize}
    \item A* path computation,
    \item waypoint synthesis,
    \item CFMS invocation and 4D trajectory generation, and
    \item the planner-stage subtotal per request.
\end{itemize}

The stage times are measured on the server inside the planner pipeline; they are not the latency that a client sees. The A* and waypoint-synthesis times cover only these computations. Trajectory generation is measured as one block, from calling the CFMS module until the dense trajectory is returned. We did not measure inside CFMS, so we always call this stage \emph{CFMS invocation and dense trajectory generation} and make no claim about the CFMS computation alone. The planner-stage subtotal is the sum of A*, waypoint synthesis, and CFMS invocation and dense trajectory generation. It does not include HTTP transport, application programming interface (API) client overhead, JavaScript Object Notation (JSON) serialization, plotting, storing results, or building the response.

With these measurements we can see how much routing, waypoint synthesis and trajectory generation each contribute.

\paragraph{Experiment B: conflict-resolution-enabled evaluation.}
The second experiment uses the same Hamburg network, aircraft assumptions and local deployment, but with conflict checking and lateral re-routing enabled. A new batch of 100 origin--destination requests was drawn from the same set of directed pairs, using the default request-pair seed 0 of the evaluation script. The requests were handled one after another. Every accepted request was added to the peer set and passed on to all later requests, so each new candidate was checked against all trajectories accepted before it. A request was only accepted if it ended without conflict---either at the first check or after re-routing. Requests that reached the retry limit or ran out of routes were rejected and did not become peers.

Each request got an absolute take-off time, drawn uniformly within a scheduling window, so that all trajectories share one time axis for NDMap. The baseline run spreads the requests over a full 24-hour UTC day. Three more runs use exactly the same OD sequence and seeds, but squeeze the window to 15, 30 and 60 minutes, so that more flights overlap in time. All four runs are therefore one and the same traffic sample viewed with different time windows, not four independent samples. For every request, the Planning Service generated a candidate trajectory, stored it, and asked the Deconfliction Service to compare it with the peer set. Without conflicts, the candidate was accepted and used as a peer for later requests. With conflicts, the Planning Service mapped them to edges (Section~\ref{sec:conflict_resolution}), removed these edges from the request-local graph, and computed a new route and trajectory. At most five planning attempts were allowed.

For every request we recorded the final status, the number of attempts, the blocked directed edges, the peer-set size, the number of peers that overlap in time (their take-off to arrival window overlaps with the candidate's), the first and final route with distances and the resulting detour ratio, a simple encounter type for the conflicting peers (head-on, same direction, or crossing at a node, derived from the blocked edge and the direction of the peer on it), the server-side stage times, the planner and conflict-detection time per attempt, and the total request time seen by the client.

\paragraph{Deterministic encounter cases.}
Besides the random runs, a script sends five checks covering four distinct encounter setups through the same running services: a head-on encounter on the same route; a follower on the same route \SI{15}{s} behind the peer; a crossing where both aircraft pass the same graph node at the same time (timed with the measured node-passage times, which also tests conflicts close to a node); a merge where two flights land at the same vertiport at the same time; and a repeat of the shared-destination setup with an explicit non-resolution assertion. The first four checks assert that an initial conflict is detected; their final resolution statuses are recorded observations. The crossing check also records whether the mapped conflict is near the node. The fifth check asserts non-resolution. It repeats the same geometry and timing and is not independent scenario evidence.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

The evaluation is kept to a controlled benchmark on purpose. The network has 29 Hamburg vertiports, requests are handled one after another, and everything runs on a single machine without distributed deployment. The benchmark uses a fixed \SI{200}{m} MSL cruise layer and does not model terrain, weather, obstacles, no-fly zones, dynamic traffic, holding, or altitude-layer assignment. We also do not compare with other route planners or trajectory generators.

Conflict detection has its own limitations, which come from the sampled check of Section~\ref{sec:conflict_detection}. The separation condition is checked at discrete times with linear interpolation between samples, not by computing the exact closest approach of two segments, and violations shorter than \SI{1}{s} are not reported. There is therefore no formal guarantee that every conflict in continuous time is found: brief violations, including grazing horizontal encounters and short vertical-threshold crossings, can be missed. Separation is also only checked against the nominal trajectory; no buffer for navigation, surveillance or wind uncertainty is added, so the thresholds are applied to an ideal path. Finally, purely vertical trajectory segments reach the detector with a lower time resolution, so the timing of vertical threshold crossings near a vertiport is less precise than along horizontal segments.

The traffic-density runs have one more limitation. They all use one fixed OD sequence, order and schedule-time seed. They show how our sequential loop behaves when the time window gets shorter, but they do not estimate conflict probabilities in general; for that, many runs with different seeds would be needed.

The results should therefore be seen as a baseline of the computational behavior of our planner pipeline and its re-routing loop, and not as an operational capacity assessment.
