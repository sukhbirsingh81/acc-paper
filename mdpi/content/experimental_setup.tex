\subsection{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:experimental_setup}

\subsubsection{Scenario and Network Model}
\label{sec:scenario_setup}

All experiments were conducted on an exemplary Hamburg vertiport network. The vertiport locations are based on the Hamburg UAM scenario investigated in previous DLR studies within the HorizonUAM project~\cite{abdellaoui2025_airspace_uam,schuchardt2023_atm_uam_review}; the corridor connections between these vertiports were defined for this study to obtain a connected benchmark graph. Vertiports are represented as graph nodes corresponding to averaged pad coordinates, and air corridors are modeled as undirected edges forming a connected graph. Each corridor operates at a fixed absolute cruise layer of \SI{200}{m} above mean sea level (MSL). This fixed-altitude, obstacle-free representation is used explicitly as a benchmark abstraction for reproducible computational evaluation.

Terrain elevation and obstacle variation are therefore not modeled in this study, and the corridor layer is defined in an MSL reference to ensure comparability across experiments. Edge weights are defined by the geodesic distance between connected vertiports, computed using the Haversine formula. Accordingly, the routing cost function is purely two-dimensional and distance-based: no additional penalties or constraints for altitude variation, no-fly areas, turn costs, weather effects, or other operational factors are included in the graph search objective.

This abstraction enables reproducible shortest-path routing while preserving the geographical layout of the exemplary network. Figure~\ref{fig:hamburg_corridor_network} shows the Hamburg vertiport corridor network used for the computational evaluation.

\subsubsection{System Configuration}
\label{sec:system_configuration}

All experiments were executed on a workstation equipped with an Intel Core i7-8650U central processing unit (CPU) at 1.90\,GHz with 4 cores and 8 threads, and 16\,GB of random-access memory (RAM) under a 64-bit operating system.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Operating system:} Ubuntu 24.04 LTS (Noble Numbat).
    \item \textbf{CFMS version:} CFMS project version~1.0.
    \item \textbf{NDMap version:} Used inside the Deconfliction Service; no standalone package release is declared for the evaluated version.
\end{itemize}

The Planning Service and CFMS trajectory generator were executed locally on the same machine. The Deconfliction Service was available in the same local deployment, although the planner-stage benchmark reported below disables deconfliction to isolate routing and trajectory-generation cost. No distributed deployment, containerization, or artificial network latency was introduced. Consequently, the reported runtimes represent baseline computational performance under single-machine execution rather than distributed service latency.

The system follows a service-oriented separation of concerns; however, the evaluated deployment does not constitute a fully distributed multi-host deployment. Therefore, while the architecture supports modular decomposition, actual horizontal scaling across multiple hosts would require additional deployment mechanisms such as shared trajectory-state management, coordinated conflict-detection data access, and inter-service orchestration.

\subsubsection{Planner, Generator, and Deconfliction Configuration}
\label{sec:planner_configuration}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Global planner:} The Planning Service performs A* search on the undirected corridor graph using Haversine distance both as edge cost and as admissible heuristic,
    \[
        f(n) = g(n) + h(n).
    \]
    The optimization objective is therefore purely 2D shortest-path distance between vertiports; altitude, no-fly constraints, and other operational cost terms are not part of the route cost model.

    \item \textbf{Deconfliction:} The Deconfliction Service invokes NDMap after CFMS-based 4D trajectory generation to evaluate the candidate trajectory against the set of already retained locally deconflicted trajectories. NDMap is not part of the Planning Service. In the implemented framework, the Deconfliction Service performs conflict detection and returns conflict information; the Planning Service performs request-local graph modification and lateral re-routing.

    \item \textbf{Cruise altitude:} A fixed absolute cruise layer of \SI{200}{m} MSL is used, independent of local pad elevation, terrain variation, and obstacle constraints. This is a benchmark assumption rather than an operational airspace model.

    \item \textbf{Waypoint synthesis:} Before CFMS execution, the planner converts each corridor route into a sparse waypoint sequence using a simplified geometry inspired by the obstacle-free-volume (OFV, ``verticone'') definitions in the EASA PTS-VPT-DSN guidance for vertical take-off and landing procedures~\cite{easa2022_pts_vpt_dsn}. For a multi-node route, the nominal sequence is:
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item origin pad waypoint,
        \item vertical climb to \(h_1\),
        \item centerline transition waypoint at \(h_2\),
        \item optional outer-limitation-surface (OLS) waypoint up to \SI{152}{m} above the pad, capped by cruise altitude,
        \item intermediate corridor nodes with altitude assigned by the design climb gradient until the fixed cruise layer is reached,
        \item optional top-of-descent waypoint on the inbound centerline,
        \item OFV entry waypoint at \(h_2\),
        \item over-pad waypoint at \(h_1\),
        \item landing decision point (LDP), and
        \item destination pad waypoint.
    \end{enumerate}
    For direct origin--destination routes, the corridor-node sequence is replaced by a single intermediate great-circle waypoint between departure and arrival procedures. The terminal geometry is therefore a rule-based construction inspired by the EASA vertiport procedure definitions adopted in this study, while the en-route corridor graph is treated as a centerline route reference rather than as an explicitly validated swept-volume corridor envelope.

    \item \textbf{Trajectory generation:} CFMS-based 4D trajectory generation is performed using phase-dependent nominal speeds derived from aircraft-performance limits and scaled to preserve operational margins.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Evaluation Methodology}
\label{sec:evaluation_methodology}

\paragraph{Experiment A: planner-stage latency decomposition and route-length dependence.}
To evaluate baseline computational performance, one batch of trajectory requests was processed sequentially through the Planning Service with deconfliction disabled. The experiment varies only the selected path distance on one fixed 29-node network; network size, concurrency, and request load are not varied. It should therefore be interpreted as a latency decomposition and a route-length-dependence analysis on a fixed network, not as a scalability or throughput benchmark.

For this analysis, 100 origin--destination vertiport pairs were sampled without replacement from the directed set of possible pairs using the benchmark script's default RNG seed 0 and recorded in the stored benchmark comma-separated-values (CSV) file. Duplicate vertiport entries and dataset entries without corridor connectivity in the evaluated network were excluded from the random sample, yielding the 29-node Hamburg network summarized in Table~\ref{tab:network_stats}. The origin and destination were always distinct, and no repeated origin--destination pair occurred in the benchmark set. Five unrecorded warmup requests preceded the measurements, and each OD pair was measured three times, yielding 300 recorded samples; all requests completed successfully, so no failed route generations were excluded from the reported results. Each trajectory request was fully completed before the next was initiated, ensuring that measured runtimes were not influenced by parallel processing effects. All timings use monotonic high-resolution clocks (\texttt{time.perf\_counter} on both client and server). The experiments ran on the laptop-class CPU of Section~\ref{sec:system_configuration} with the operating-system default frequency scaling; no CPU-governor pinning or turbo-boost control was applied, which contributes to the reported run-to-run variance. Reported quantiles use the nearest-rank (floor) method on the sorted sample, and the per-bucket sample counts \(n\) are annotated in the distance-bucket figures.

Runtime measurements were recorded separately for:
\begin{itemize}
    \item A* path computation,
    \item waypoint synthesis,
    \item CFMS invocation and 4D trajectory generation, and
    \item the planner-stage subtotal per request.
\end{itemize}

The reported stage timings are server-side measurements of the planner pipeline, not client-perceived service latency. A* and waypoint-synthesis timings cover only the corresponding internal computations. The trajectory-generation stage is measured as one block covering the in-process CFMS module invocation and retrieval of the dense generated trajectory; the engine's internal computation is not instrumented separately, so this stage is consistently referred to as \emph{CFMS invocation and dense trajectory generation}, and no claim is made about the engine-internal computation alone. The planner-stage subtotal is the sum of A*, waypoint synthesis, and CFMS invocation and dense trajectory generation; it does not include HTTP transport, application-programming-interface (API) client overhead, request or response JavaScript Object Notation (JSON) serialization and deserialization, plotting, visualization rendering, artifact persistence, or response construction.

These measurements provide insight into the relative computational contributions of routing, waypoint synthesis, and trajectory generation within the proposed framework.

\paragraph{Experiment B: conflict-resolution-enabled evaluation.}
The conflict-resolution-enabled experiment uses the same Hamburg network, aircraft assumptions, and local service deployment, but enables both conflict checking and lateral re-routing. A second batch of 100 origin--destination requests was sampled from the same directed candidate set using the evaluation script's default request-pair seed of 0. Requests were processed sequentially. After each locally deconflicted request, its trajectory identifier was retained in the active peer set and passed to all later requests; therefore, each new candidate trajectory was checked against all previously retained trajectories in the run.

Each request was assigned an absolute schedule time sampled uniformly within a scheduling window so that all 4D trajectories shared a common time axis for NDMap. The baseline run uses the full 24-hour UTC day; three additional traffic-density scenarios reuse the identical OD sequence and seeds but compress the scheduling window to 15, 30, and 60 minutes, increasing the probability of temporal overlap between requests. For every request, the Planning Service generated a candidate trajectory, persisted the trajectory samples, and invoked the Deconfliction Service to compare the candidate with the peer set. If no conflicts were reported, the candidate was retained as a locally deconflicted plan for subsequent peer checks. If conflicts were reported, the Planning Service applied the conflict-to-edge mapping of Section~\ref{sec:conflict_resolution}, removed the mapped edge from the request-local graph, and recomputed the route and trajectory. The maximum number of planning attempts was five.

Per request, the evaluation recorded the final conflict-resolution status, the number of attempts, the blocked directed edges, the peer-set size, the number of temporally overlapping peers (peers whose take-off--arrival window intersects the candidate's), the initial and final route with distances and the resulting detour ratio, a heuristic encounter-type classification of the conflicting peers (head-on, same-direction, or node-crossing, derived from the blocked directed edge and the peer's route direction on it), the server-side stage timings, the per-attempt planner and conflict-detection latencies, and the client-observed end-to-end latency.

\paragraph{Deterministic encounter cases.}
In addition to the randomized runs, a scripted suite constructs five deterministic encounter geometries with known expected outcomes through the same running services: a same-route head-on encounter, a same-route in-trail (following) encounter \SI{15}{s} behind the peer, a crossing encounter timed---using measured node-passage epochs---so that candidate and peer pass the same intermediate graph node simultaneously (which also probes conflicts near a graph node), a merge encounter with two flights scheduled to land at the same vertiport at the same time, and an encounter that lateral re-routing cannot resolve because every lateral alternative still terminates at the same pad at the same landing time. Each case asserts its expected detector and resolution outcome.

\subsubsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

The evaluation is intentionally limited to a controlled benchmark setting. The network contains 29 Hamburg vertiport nodes, requests are processed sequentially, and the planner benchmark is executed locally on a single machine without distributed deployment effects. The benchmark uses a fixed \SI{200}{m} MSL cruise layer and does not model terrain, weather, obstacles, no-fly zones, dynamic traffic, temporal holding, or altitude-layer assignment. It also does not compare alternative route planners or trajectory generators.

Conflict detection carries its own limitations, which follow from the sampled evaluation described in Section~\ref{sec:conflict_detection}. The separation predicate is applied at discrete probe instants with linear interpolation between trajectory samples, not by solving analytically for the closest approach of two trajectory segments, and violations shorter than \SI{1}{s} are discarded. Continuous-time detection completeness is therefore not formally guaranteed: grazing encounters whose minimum separation lies marginally inside the horizontal threshold can remain unreported. Separation is also assessed against the nominal generated trajectory only; no navigation, surveillance, or wind uncertainty buffer is added around it, so the thresholds are applied to an idealized deterministic path. Finally, the detector's internal representation drops trajectory samples that repeat the preceding latitude and longitude within \SI{3}{s}, so pad-vertical segments are represented by their endpoints and interpolated linearly, which reduces vertical fidelity in the immediate vicinity of a vertiport.

The results should therefore be read as a baseline computational characterization of the implemented planner pipeline and its lateral-re-routing conflict-resolution loop rather than as a complete operational capacity assessment.
