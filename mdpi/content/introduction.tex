\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Urban Air Mobility (UAM) is emerging as a promising extension of future transportation systems. To enable the safe integration of large numbers of electric vertical take-off and landing (eVTOL) aircraft into low-altitude airspace, structured traffic-management frameworks such as the European U-space~\cite{eu_commission_2017,easa2020uspace} and the U.S. UAS Traffic Management (UTM) concept~\cite{kopardekar2016utm} have been proposed. These initiatives emphasize service-oriented architectures and trajectory-based operations to support scalable and safe airspace integration~\cite{sesar_conops_2019,sesar_master_plan_2025}. It should be noted that the U-space framework itself does not prescribe corridors: U-space services are provided within designated regions or volumes, and unmanned aircraft system (UAS) operators can plan their trajectories within those volumes. Nevertheless, UAM corridors are envisioned by some operators for early UAM implementation, with U-space services provided for a larger volume covering all UAM corridors of a region, as investigated, for example, in the SESAR project EUREKA~\cite{sesar_eureka_project}. This paper therefore addresses structured corridor operations within U-space rather than treating U-space as a corridor concept.

A central requirement in such environments is computationally efficient path planning. In dense urban scenarios, trajectory requests must be processed while respecting separation requirements and structured corridor constraints. Search-based approaches such as A* have been widely investigated for UAS routing~\cite{sahawneh2016_3d_path_planning,a_star_2d3d_comparison_utm}, alongside four-dimensional (4D) grid-based conflict management strategies~\cite{acevedo_4d_grids_u_space}. Deconfliction in multi-dimensional trajectory spaces has also been studied in trajectory-based operations frameworks~\cite{NDMap,enea_porretta_4d_tbo_comparison}, and surveys summarize the broader range of UAS path-planning methods~\cite{ahmad2023_uav_path_planning_survey}.

In operational U-space systems, path planning is not an isolated routing problem. A selected route must be converted into a time-parameterized 4D trajectory, checked against operational constraints, and prepared as candidate operation intent for a U-space authorization workflow. In this paper, \emph{candidate operation intent} denotes a locally prepared, versioned description of an intended operation---origin and destination, time window, and 4D trajectory---that an operator would submit to a USSP for authorization; the submitted form is referred to as a U-plan. Monolithic implementations of these functions may limit extensibility and independent scaling. Microservice architectures offer a modular alternative by separating concerns such as topology provision, trajectory generation, deconfliction, and U-space-facing plan submission into dedicated software services~\cite{dragoni2017microservices,matlekovic2020uav}. However, quantitative evaluations of service-oriented U-space planning systems remain limited, particularly with respect to latency decomposition, the dependence of planning cost on route length, and the evaluated behavior of integrated conflict-checking and re-planning loops.

This paper presents a service-oriented 4D path-planning architecture for structured corridor operations within U-space and evaluates both the planner-stage latency decomposition, including its route-length dependence, and conflict-enabled behavior. Two existing tools of the German Aerospace Center (DLR) are used as building blocks. The Copter Flight Management System (CFMS) is a modular experimental flight-management and 4D trajectory generation system for unmanned multicopter and other vertical take-off and landing (VTOL) aircraft that converts a phase-structured waypoint sequence into a dense, kinematically consistent, time-parameterized trajectory~\cite{morscheck2021cfms}. NDMap is a high-performance conflict-detection library for multi-dimensional objects that indexes trajectories in a combined space--time state space and reports separation violations between them~\cite{NDMap}. The architecture separates graph-based route planning, waypoint synthesis, 4D trajectory generation, NDMap-based deconfliction, and a U-space Service Provider (USSP)-facing authorization boundary into distinct service responsibilities. In this architecture, the Planning and Deconfliction Services prepare candidate 4D intent; they do not themselves authorize U-space operations. The planner stage combines A*-based routing on a vertiport corridor graph with CFMS-based trajectory generation~\cite{morscheck2021cfms}.

The evaluated planning pipeline follows four main steps. First, A* selects a shortest corridor route on the vertiport graph. Second, waypoint synthesis augments this route with departure, climb, cruise, descent, and landing structure. Third, CFMS converts the structured waypoint sequence into a dense time-parameterized 4D trajectory. Fourth, when conflict checking is enabled, NDMap checks the candidate trajectory against previously retained peer trajectories and conflict intervals are fed back to the Planning Service for request-local lateral re-routing. Each planning request in this paper corresponds to a single operator request for candidate trajectory generation for one origin--destination vertiport pair. Operational flight authorization by a USSP is treated as a downstream boundary and is not evaluated in the experiments.

The evaluation is organized around three research questions:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=3.6em,labelwidth=3em,align=left]
    \item[\textbf{RQ1}] How does planner-stage latency decompose across A* route search, waypoint synthesis, and CFMS invocation and dense trajectory generation, and how does it depend on selected route length on a fixed corridor network?
    \item[\textbf{RQ2}] Under sequential operation with an accumulating peer-trajectory set, how often do NDMap-detected conflicts occur as a function of schedule-time density, and can request-local lateral re-routing resolve them?
    \item[\textbf{RQ3}] When conflict checking and re-routing are enabled, where does end-to-end request latency go---planning, conflict detection, or service overhead?
\end{itemize}

The contributions of this work are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item A service-oriented U-space path-planning architecture that separates topology provision, planning, trajectory generation, deconfliction, and USSP-facing authorization-boundary responsibilities.
    \item A CFMS-compatible waypoint-synthesis pipeline that converts A*-selected corridor routes into phase-structured inputs for 4D trajectory generation; this pipeline is an implementation contribution that enables the benchmark rather than a claimed algorithmic novelty.
    \item A planner-stage latency decomposition into A* path computation, waypoint synthesis, and CFMS invocation and dense trajectory generation, including its route-length dependence (RQ1).
    \item An NDMap-based conflict-checking loop with request-local lateral re-routing by graph-edge removal and A* re-planning, evaluated with randomized and deterministic encounter scenarios (RQ2) and an end-to-end latency decomposition (RQ3).
\end{itemize}
