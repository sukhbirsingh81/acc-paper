\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{BLAN--WOHL Trajectory Case}
\label{sec:blan_wohl_trajectory}

This section first presents one representative trajectory case to illustrate the output of the planner pipeline, followed by the runtime and conflict-resolution results for the benchmark requests. To illustrate the route and trajectory-generation result produced by the Planning Service, we use the request with take-off vertiport \texttt{BLAN} and landing vertiport \texttt{WOHL}. The request uses the fixed cruise layer of \SI{200}{m} MSL and does not invoke the Deconfliction Service; deconfliction is treated as a separate service boundary in this architecture. This case was selected because it traverses multiple corridor segments and clearly separates departure, cruise, and arrival phases. For this origin--destination pair, the A* planner selects the corridor sequence
\[
\texttt{BLAN} \rightarrow \texttt{VOLK} \rightarrow \texttt{SCHN} \rightarrow
\texttt{FUHB} \rightarrow \texttt{WOHL}.
\]

Figure~\ref{fig:blan_wohl_paths_overview} shows this route in the context of the Hamburg corridor graph. The thinner blue edges represent available corridor connections, the red markers show graph nodes, and the thicker blue polyline highlights the selected A* path from \texttt{BLAN} to \texttt{WOHL}. This overview makes the global routing result visible before the route is expanded into a detailed 4D trajectory.

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{figures/blan_wohl_paths_overview.png}
  \caption{A* corridor path overview for the \texttt{BLAN} to \texttt{WOHL} request. The highlighted path connects the departure vertiport \texttt{BLAN} with the landing vertiport \texttt{WOHL} through the selected corridor sequence via the intermediate vertiports \texttt{VOLK}, \texttt{SCHN}, and \texttt{FUHB}.}
  \label{fig:blan_wohl_paths_overview}
\end{figure}

The waypoint synthesizer expands this five-node route into 11 CFMS input waypoints, including departure, transition, cruise, top-of-descent, and landing waypoints. The three internal cruise-node waypoints corresponding to \texttt{VOLK}, \texttt{SCHN}, and \texttt{FUHB} are retained as centerline route references for CFMS input. The resulting dense trajectory contains 1713 samples at approximately \SI{1}{s} spacing, covers a cumulative along-track distance of \SI{27.99}{km}, and has a total duration of \SI{1596.74}{s}, or \SI{26.61}{min}.

The speed field reported with each trajectory sample is the horizontal component of the aircraft speed: it tracks the rate of change of the cumulative along-track distance and is exactly zero throughout the purely vertical climb and descent segments, where the aircraft is nevertheless moving vertically. It is therefore referred to as \emph{horizontal speed} in this paper. Because no wind field is modeled in the benchmark, horizontal airspeed and horizontal ground speed coincide numerically here and cannot be distinguished from the generated data; the vertical component is not included in the field and its decomposition is not evaluated. The unweighted mean of the horizontal-speed samples over this trajectory is \SI{17.15}{m/s}, whereas cumulative distance divided by duration gives \SI{17.53}{m/s}. The difference is a sampling artifact, not a physical one: the samples are not uniformly spaced in time---the terminal phases are sampled more densely than cruise---so the unweighted sample mean over-weights the slow terminal segments. Weighting each sample by its time interval reproduces the distance-over-duration value of \SI{17.53}{m/s} exactly. The cruise segment holds approximately \SI{25}{m/s}.

Figure~\ref{fig:blan_wohl_map} separates the lateral trajectory into three phase panels. The phase boundaries are defined by the corridor route: the first panel, labeled \emph{departure and outbound climb}, spans from the origin pad to the first corridor node and therefore includes the full outbound corridor leg to \texttt{VOLK}, not only the terminal take-off procedure; analogously, the \emph{inbound descent and arrival} panel spans from the last corridor node \texttt{FUHB} to the destination pad, including the full inbound leg. The initial climb is flown essentially vertically over the departure pad, so the lateral track leaves \texttt{BLAN} directly along the outbound corridor direction without a low-height turning maneuver around the pad. The cruise panel shows the route through the selected corridor nodes \texttt{VOLK}, \texttt{SCHN}, and \texttt{FUHB}; the arrival panel ends in a vertical final descent over the destination pad. The figure therefore confirms that the generated trajectory follows the A*-selected corridor sequence while adding the terminal geometry required at both endpoints. The corresponding vertical and horizontal-speed profiles are shown in Figures~\ref{fig:blan_wohl_altitude} and~\ref{fig:blan_wohl_speed}.

Figure~\ref{fig:blan_wohl_altitude} shows the vertical profile of the generated trajectory. The three panels use local phase-relative time axes rather than one continuous global time axis, so each phase can be inspected at an appropriate temporal scale. During departure and outbound climb, the trajectory first climbs vertically from the pad through the lower terminal height and then climbs steeply along the outbound leg to the outer-limitation-surface reference at \SI{152}{m}, which it holds until reaching the first corridor node below the cruise layer. The aircraft then reaches and maintains the \SI{200}{m} MSL cruise layer during the central corridor segment. On arrival, the trajectory descends from the cruise layer to the \SI{152}{m} approach reference shortly after leaving the last corridor segment, holds this level on the inbound leg until the top of descent, and then descends through the \SI{60}{m} and \SI{15}{m} terminal references before a vertical final descent onto the \texttt{WOHL} pad. This profile illustrates how the waypoint-synthesis logic combines the fixed cruise altitude with structured vertiport departure and arrival procedures.

Figure~\ref{fig:blan_wohl_speed} shows the corresponding horizontal-speed profile. As in the altitude profile, the panels use local phase-relative time axes rather than one global time axis. The profile starts and ends at zero because the initial climb and final descent are flown vertically over the pads. After the vertical lift-off, the aircraft flies the departure funnel segment at a low constant speed of approximately \SI{5}{m/s}, accelerates to \SI{20}{m/s} on the outbound corridor leg, and reaches the cruise setting of approximately \SI{25}{m/s} along the central corridor segment. On arrival, the speed is reduced in stages through the approach speed limits near the destination vertiport before the final vertical descent. Explicit swept-volume corridor-containment validation remains a downstream check rather than a claim made from this speed profile alone.

\subsection{Planner-Stage Latency Decomposition and Route-Length Dependence}
\label{sec:planner_scalability}

This experiment evaluates the Planning Service in isolation from the Deconfliction Service. It measures a sequential baseline over a fixed batch of requests and the dependence of latency on selected path distance on one fixed network; it is not a concurrent-throughput or network-size scalability benchmark. A batch of \paPairs{} randomly sampled origin--destination pairs was processed synchronously, each pair measured \paReps{} times after five unrecorded warmup requests, yielding \paObs{} timing observations over \paPairs{} distinct route geometries. Each request used the Hamburg corridor graph, the fixed \SI{200}{m} MSL cruise layer, and disabled deconfliction. The benchmark pairs were sampled without replacement, each pair had distinct origin and destination vertiports, and no origin--destination pair was repeated. All requests completed successfully, so no failed cases were removed before computing the reported statistics. As set out in Section~\ref{sec:evaluation_methodology}, the runtime distributions below use all \paObs{} timing observations, whereas every regression, confidence interval and correlation uses the \paPairs{} OD-level means.

Table~\ref{tab:planner_scalability_runtime} summarizes the measured runtime distribution for the planner-relevant stages. We use the term \emph{planner-stage subtotal} for the sum of A* path computation, waypoint synthesis, and CFMS invocation and dense trajectory generation. This subtotal has a mean of \SI{\paPlannerMean}{ms}, a \(p_{90}\) latency of \SI{\paPlannerPninety}{ms}, and a maximum of \SI{\paPlannerMax}{ms} over the \paObs{} timing observations. This latency range is compatible with interactive planning and rapid route preview in the evaluated local setup, without implying operational readiness under distributed or concurrent traffic conditions. CFMS invocation and dense trajectory generation dominate this subtotal with a mean of \SI{\paGenMean}{ms}. In contrast, waypoint synthesis and A* path search remain sub-millisecond, with maxima of \SI{\paWpMax}{ms} and \SI{\paAstarMax}{ms}, respectively.

CFMS invocation and dense trajectory generation account for almost the entire planner-stage runtime, while A* and waypoint synthesis are negligible in comparison. Using the mean values in Table~\ref{tab:planner_scalability_runtime}, this stage contributes approximately \paGenShare{}\% of the planner-stage subtotal. Because this stage dominates the subtotal, the route-length dependence of the subtotal follows the route-length dependence of the trajectory-generation stage.

Figure~\ref{fig:planner_scalability_distance} relates the planner-stage runtimes to selected path distance over the \paPairs{} OD-level aggregates. The planner-stage subtotal and the trajectory-generation stage both increase strongly with distance (Pearson \(r = \paPlannerDistR{}\) and \(r = \paGenDistR{}\)). A linear regression of the planner-stage subtotal on path distance yields a slope of \SI{\paPlannerDistSlope}{ms/km} (95\% confidence interval \([\paPlannerDistLo{}, \paPlannerDistHi{}]\), \(R^2 = \paPlannerDistRsq{}\), \(n = \paPairs{}\) OD pairs). The mechanism is resolved by two further regressions: the trajectory sample count grows almost perfectly linearly with distance (\paSamplesDistSlope{} samples per km, 95\% CI \([\paSamplesDistLo{}, \paSamplesDistHi{}]\), \(R^2 = \paSamplesDistRsq{}\)), and the trajectory-generation time grows linearly with the sample count (\SI{\paGenSamplesSlope}{ms} per sample, 95\% CI \([\paGenSamplesLo{}, \paGenSamplesHi{}]\), \(R^2 = \paGenSamplesRsq{}\)). Longer routes therefore cost more primarily because they produce proportionally more dense trajectory samples. Waypoint synthesis (Pearson \(r = \paWpDistR{}\)) and A* path search (Pearson \(r = \paAstarDistR{}\)) remain small in absolute terms across the evaluated distance range. This indicates that, for the evaluated network, route search is not the limiting factor.

\subsection{Conflict-Resolution-Enabled Evaluation}
\label{sec:conflict_resolution_results}

A second sequential batch of \cfRequests{} trajectory requests was evaluated with conflict checking and lateral re-routing enabled. All reported conflict-enabled experiments use the validated detector configuration and closest-approach reporting described in Section~\ref{sec:conflict_detection}. In this run, every locally deconflicted trajectory was retained as a peer trajectory for later requests. For each new request, the Planning Service first generated a candidate 4D trajectory and then submitted it to the Deconfliction Service; reported conflicts were mapped to route edges and resolved by request-local lateral re-routing as described in Section~\ref{sec:conflict_resolution}, until the trajectory was conflict-free or the configured retry limit of five attempts was reached.

\paragraph{Full-day baseline.}
All \cfRequests{} submitted candidate-planning requests were retained as locally deconflicted plans: \cfClear{} were conflict-free on the first deconfliction check, one---the first request in the sequence---had an empty peer set, and \cfResolved{} were resolved by lateral re-routing. Under this full-day baseline, pairwise temporal overlap was sparse---\cfOverlapPairs{} of \cfComparisons{} candidate--peer pairs overlapped---although \cfWithOverlap{} of the \cfWithPeers{} requests with a non-empty peer set had at least one temporally overlapping peer. Actual conflicts nevertheless remained rare: \cfConflicted{} of those \cfWithOverlap{} requests (\cfConflictRate{}\%) had an initial conflict, so even among pairs that share a flight window, most are separated in space at every common instant. The \cfResolved{} resolved requests required \cfAttemptList{} planning attempts, blocked \cfBlockedEdges{} request-local directed edges in total, and accepted detour ratios of \cfDetourList{} relative to their initial shortest routes. No request reached the retry limit, no mapping stall occurred, and no routing error occurred. Because only \cfResolved{} requests encountered initial conflicts, the baseline should be read as evidence that the loop functions, not as a statistical estimate of resolution performance. Figure~\ref{fig:conflict_resolution_eval} summarizes the outcomes and attempt counts; Table~\ref{tab:conflict_resolution_cases} reports every conflict case individually.

\paragraph{End-to-end latency decomposition.}
Figure~\ref{fig:conflict_latency_decomposition} decomposes the client-observed end-to-end request time into planning (including artifact persistence), conflict detection, and HTTP/serialization overhead, separately for first-pass conflict-free requests and for resolved requests. Conflict-free requests average \cfClearClientMs{}~ms end to end, of which only \cfClearPlanMs{}~ms is planning and \cfClearOverheadMs{}~ms is transport overhead; the remaining \cfClearDetectMs{}~ms is the single conflict-detection call. Resolved requests average \cfResolvedClientMs{}~ms, with \cfResolvedPlanMs{}~ms of accumulated planning, \cfResolvedDetectMs{}~ms of accumulated detection over their attempts, and \cfResolvedOverheadMs{}~ms of transport overhead. The detection cost is architectural rather than algorithmic: as documented in Section~\ref{sec:conflict_detection}, every check re-fetches the candidate and all referenced peer trajectories---up to 99 of them late in the run, each a dense trajectory of order one thousand samples---from the trajectory store and rebuilds the detector state. This is why the end-to-end time of conflict-checked requests exceeds the planner-stage subtotal of Section~\ref{sec:planner_scalability} by more than an order of magnitude, and it identifies peer re-fetching and detector-state rebuilding, not trajectory generation, as the dominant cost of the conflict-checked path.

\paragraph{Traffic-density scenarios.}
Table~\ref{tab:conflict_density} repeats the identical request sequence with the schedule-time window compressed from the full day to 60, 30, and 15 minutes. All four scenarios share one fixed OD sequence, admission order and schedule-time seed, so they compare one realization against itself under time-window compression rather than sampling a traffic population. Conflict frequency rises monotonically with density, and the dense windows expose the outcome classes that the sparse baseline cannot: retry-limit exhaustion and no-route outcomes, in which lateral re-routing runs out of alternatives because every remaining route is blocked or the request-local graph becomes disconnected at the origin. Mapping stalls did not occur in any run. In this fixed-seed realization, the fraction of requests with an initial conflict among requests having at least one temporally overlapping peer increased from \cfConflictRate{}\% (full day) to \cfFifteenRate{}\% (15-minute window). These fractions describe the evaluated realization and are not estimates of a population-level conflict probability. Even in the densest window the number of temporally overlapping pairs exceeds the number of reported conflicts by a wide margin, because overlap in time only selects the pairs that are examined co-temporally. Median accepted detours for resolved requests remain moderate (between \cfDenseDetourMedianMin{} and \cfDenseDetourMedianMax{} in the dense windows), with individual worst cases up to a detour ratio of \cfDenseDetourMax{}.

\paragraph{Deterministic encounter cases.}
Table~\ref{tab:deterministic_cases} lists the five scripted encounter geometries and their observed outcomes; all match the expected behavior. Opposite-direction and timed node-crossing encounters are detected and resolved laterally, and the crossing conflict is confirmed to lie within \SI{556}{m} of the shared graph node---a check that exercises the reported closest-approach time directly, since the conflict is located by mapping that time onto the candidate trajectory. A same-route follower \SI{15}{s} in trail conflicts along every lateral alternative and ends with an exhausted request-local graph (no route). The two shared-destination cases likewise terminate with no route left: the conflict maps to the arrival edge into the shared pad, and the alternatives into that pad are exhausted within three attempts. These cases delimit what the implemented lateral-only strategy can and cannot resolve.
