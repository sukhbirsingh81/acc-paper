\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{BLAN--WOHL Trajectory Case}
\label{sec:blan_wohl_trajectory}

This section first presents one representative trajectory case to illustrate the output of the planner pipeline, followed by the runtime and conflict-resolution results for the benchmark requests. To illustrate the route and trajectory-generation result produced by the Planning Service, we use the request with take-off vertiport \texttt{BLAN} and landing vertiport \texttt{WOHL}. The request uses the fixed cruise layer of \SI{200}{m} MSL and does not invoke the Deconfliction Service; deconfliction is treated as a separate service boundary in this architecture. This case was selected because it traverses multiple corridor segments and clearly separates departure, cruise, and arrival phases. For this origin--destination pair, the A* planner selects the corridor sequence
\[
\texttt{BLAN} \rightarrow \texttt{VOLK} \rightarrow \texttt{SCHN} \rightarrow
\texttt{FUHB} \rightarrow \texttt{WOHL}.
\]

Figure~\ref{fig:blan_wohl_paths_overview} shows this route in the context of the Hamburg corridor graph. The thinner blue edges represent available corridor connections, the red markers show graph nodes, and the thicker blue polyline highlights the selected A* path from \texttt{BLAN} to \texttt{WOHL}. This overview makes the global routing result visible before the route is expanded into a detailed 4D trajectory.

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{figures/blan_wohl_paths_overview.png}
  \caption{A* corridor path overview for the \texttt{BLAN} to \texttt{WOHL} request. The highlighted path connects the departure vertiport \texttt{BLAN} with the landing vertiport \texttt{WOHL} through the selected corridor sequence via the intermediate vertiports \texttt{VOLK}, \texttt{SCHN}, and \texttt{FUHB}.}
  \label{fig:blan_wohl_paths_overview}
\end{figure}

The waypoint synthesizer expands this five-node route into 11 CFMS input waypoints, including departure, transition, cruise, top-of-descent, and landing waypoints. The three internal cruise-node waypoints corresponding to \texttt{VOLK}, \texttt{SCHN}, and \texttt{FUHB} are retained as centerline route references for CFMS input. The resulting dense trajectory contains 1713 samples at approximately \SI{1}{s} spacing, covers a cumulative distance of \SI{27.99}{km}, and has a total duration of \SI{1596.74}{s}, or \SI{26.61}{min}. The mean true airspeed over the generated trajectory is \SI{17.15}{m/s}; this is the sample mean over the uniformly time-spaced samples and therefore a time-weighted average, which is lower than cumulative distance divided by duration (\SI{17.53}{m/s}) because the vertical departure and arrival segments contribute flight time at near-zero airspeed but almost no distance. The cruise segment reaches approximately \SI{25.00}{m/s}.

Figure~\ref{fig:blan_wohl_map} separates the lateral trajectory into three phase panels. The phase boundaries are defined by the corridor route: the first panel, labeled \emph{departure and outbound climb}, spans from the origin pad to the first corridor node and therefore includes the full outbound corridor leg to \texttt{VOLK}, not only the terminal take-off procedure; analogously, the \emph{inbound descent and arrival} panel spans from the last corridor node \texttt{FUHB} to the destination pad, including the full inbound leg. The initial climb is flown essentially vertically over the departure pad, so the lateral track leaves \texttt{BLAN} directly along the outbound corridor direction without a low-height turning maneuver around the pad. The cruise panel shows the route through the selected corridor nodes \texttt{VOLK}, \texttt{SCHN}, and \texttt{FUHB}; the arrival panel ends in a vertical final descent over the destination pad. The figure therefore confirms that the generated trajectory follows the A*-selected corridor sequence while adding the terminal geometry required at both endpoints. The corresponding vertical and speed profiles are shown in Figures~\ref{fig:blan_wohl_altitude} and~\ref{fig:blan_wohl_tas}.

Red numbered markers indicate synthesized CFMS waypoints in sequence order; the blue line is the generated trajectory; black triangles mark each panel's start and end points. Figure~\ref{fig:blan_wohl_altitude} shows the vertical profile of the generated trajectory. The three panels use local phase-relative time axes rather than one continuous global time axis, so each phase can be inspected at an appropriate temporal scale. During departure and outbound climb, the trajectory first climbs vertically from the pad through the lower terminal height and then climbs steeply along the outbound leg to the outer-limitation-surface reference at \SI{152}{m}, which it holds until reaching the first corridor node below the cruise layer. The aircraft then reaches and maintains the \SI{200}{m} MSL cruise layer during the central corridor segment. On arrival, the trajectory descends from the cruise layer to the \SI{152}{m} approach reference shortly after leaving the last corridor segment, holds this level on the inbound leg until the top of descent, and then descends through the \SI{60}{m} and \SI{15}{m} terminal references before a vertical final descent onto the \texttt{WOHL} pad. This profile illustrates how the waypoint-synthesis logic combines the fixed cruise altitude with structured vertiport departure and arrival procedures.

Figure~\ref{fig:blan_wohl_tas} shows the corresponding true airspeed profile. As in the altitude profile, the panels use local phase-relative time axes rather than one global time axis. After the vertical lift-off, the aircraft flies the departure funnel segment at a low constant speed of approximately \SI{5}{m/s}, accelerates to \SI{20}{m/s} on the outbound corridor leg, and reaches the cruise setting of approximately \SI{25}{m/s} along the central corridor segment. On arrival, the speed is reduced in stages through the approach speed limits near the destination vertiport before the final vertical descent at essentially zero horizontal speed. Explicit swept-volume corridor-containment validation remains a downstream check rather than a claim made from this speed profile alone.

\subsection{Planner-Stage Latency Decomposition and Route-Length Dependence}
\label{sec:planner_scalability}

This experiment evaluates the Planning Service in isolation from the Deconfliction Service. It measures a sequential baseline over a fixed batch of requests and the dependence of latency on selected path distance on one fixed network; it is not a concurrent-throughput or network-size scalability benchmark. A batch of 100 randomly sampled origin--destination pairs was processed synchronously, each pair measured three times after five unrecorded warmup requests, yielding 300 recorded samples. Each request used the Hamburg corridor graph, the fixed \SI{200}{m} MSL cruise layer, and disabled deconfliction. The benchmark pairs were sampled without replacement, each pair had distinct origin and destination vertiports, and no origin--destination pair was repeated. All requests completed successfully, so no failed cases were removed before computing the reported statistics.

Table~\ref{tab:planner_scalability_runtime} summarizes the measured runtime distribution for the planner-relevant stages. We use the term \emph{planner-stage subtotal} for the sum of A* path computation, waypoint synthesis, and CFMS invocation and dense trajectory generation. This subtotal has a mean of \SI{47.25}{ms}, a \(p_{90}\) latency of \SI{73.02}{ms}, and a maximum of \SI{111.72}{ms} over the 300 samples. This latency range is compatible with interactive planning and rapid route preview in the evaluated local setup, without implying operational readiness under distributed or concurrent traffic conditions. CFMS invocation and dense trajectory generation dominate this subtotal with a mean of \SI{46.88}{ms}. In contrast, waypoint synthesis and A* path search remain sub-millisecond, with maxima of \SI{0.33}{ms} and \SI{0.76}{ms}, respectively.

CFMS invocation and dense trajectory generation account for almost the entire planner-stage runtime, while A* and waypoint synthesis are negligible in comparison. Using the mean values in Table~\ref{tab:planner_scalability_runtime}, this stage contributes approximately \(46.88/47.25 \approx 99.2\%\) of the planner-stage subtotal. Because this stage dominates the subtotal, the route-length dependence of the subtotal follows the route-length dependence of the trajectory-generation stage.

Figure~\ref{fig:planner_scalability_distance} relates the planner-stage runtimes to selected path distance. The planner-stage subtotal and the trajectory-generation stage both increase strongly with distance (Pearson \(r = 0.89\) for both). A linear regression of the planner-stage subtotal on path distance yields a slope of \SI{2.14}{ms/km} (95\% confidence interval \([2.01, 2.26]\), \(R^2 = 0.80\)). The mechanism is resolved by two further regressions: the trajectory sample count grows almost perfectly linearly with distance (47.4 samples per km, 95\% CI \([46.3, 48.6]\), \(R^2 = 0.96\)), and the trajectory-generation time grows linearly with the sample count (\SI{0.045}{ms} per sample, 95\% CI \([0.043, 0.047]\), \(R^2 = 0.84\)). Longer routes therefore cost more primarily because they produce proportionally more dense trajectory samples. Waypoint synthesis (Pearson \(r = 0.20\)) and A* path search (Pearson \(r = 0.31\)) remain small in absolute terms across the evaluated distance range. This indicates that, for the evaluated network, route search is not the limiting factor.

The distance-bucket boxplots in Figure~\ref{fig:planner_scalability_boxplots} provide the same conclusion in distributional form; each bucket label reports its sample count \(n\) (87, 147, 54, and 12 samples for the 0--10, 10--20, 20--30, and 30--40 km buckets). Median planner-stage subtotal latency and trajectory-generation latency both increase with distance bucket, while the within-bucket spread does not increase monotonically (per-bucket standard deviations of 8.4, 11.3, 12.3, and \SI{7.3}{ms}; the sparse top bucket is narrower than the middle buckets). A* latency remains tightly bounded across all distance buckets: graph search has negligible latency compared with trajectory generation. The corresponding histograms in Figure~\ref{fig:planner_scalability_hist} show that the planner-stage subtotal distribution is effectively the trajectory-generation distribution with a small sub-millisecond contribution from waypoint synthesis and graph search.

\subsection{Conflict-Resolution-Enabled Evaluation}
\label{sec:conflict_resolution_results}

A second sequential batch of \cfRequests{} trajectory requests was evaluated with conflict checking and lateral re-routing enabled, using the corrected detector of Section~\ref{sec:conflict_detection}. In this run, every locally deconflicted trajectory was retained as a peer trajectory for later requests. For each new request, the Planning Service first generated a candidate 4D trajectory and then submitted it to the Deconfliction Service; reported conflicts were mapped to route edges and resolved by request-local lateral re-routing as described in Section~\ref{sec:conflict_resolution}, until the trajectory was conflict-free or the configured retry limit of five attempts was reached.

\paragraph{Full-day baseline.}
All \cfRequests{} submitted candidate-planning requests were retained as locally deconflicted plans: \cfClear{} were conflict-free on the first deconfliction check, one---the first request in the sequence---had an empty peer set, and \cfResolved{} were resolved by lateral re-routing. In total the run performed \cfComparisons{} candidate--peer comparisons, of which \cfOverlapPairs{} pairs were temporally overlapping; \cfWithOverlap{} requests had at least one temporally overlapping peer, and \cfConflicted{} of these \cfWithOverlap{} (\cfConflictRate{}\%) had an initial conflict. Temporal overlap is therefore common while conflicts are rare: under full-day scheduling most peer pairs are separated in space at every shared instant, and overlapping flight windows only determine which pairs are examined at all. The \cfResolved{} resolved requests required \cfAttemptList{} planning attempts, blocked \cfBlockedEdges{} request-local directed edges in total, and accepted detour ratios of \cfDetourList{} relative to their initial shortest routes. No request reached the retry limit, no mapping stall occurred, and no routing error occurred. Because only \cfResolved{} requests encountered initial conflicts, the baseline should be read as evidence that the loop functions, not as a statistical estimate of resolution performance. Figure~\ref{fig:conflict_resolution_eval} summarizes the outcomes and attempt counts; Table~\ref{tab:conflict_resolution_cases} reports every conflict case individually.

\paragraph{End-to-end latency decomposition.}
Figure~\ref{fig:conflict_latency_decomposition} decomposes the client-observed end-to-end request time into planning (including artifact persistence), conflict detection, and HTTP/serialization overhead, separately for first-pass conflict-free requests and for resolved requests. Conflict-free requests average \cfClearClientMs{}~ms end to end, of which only \cfClearPlanMs{}~ms is planning and \cfClearOverheadMs{}~ms is transport overhead; the remaining \cfClearDetectMs{}~ms is the single conflict-detection call. Resolved requests average \cfResolvedClientMs{}~ms, with \cfResolvedPlanMs{}~ms of accumulated planning, \cfResolvedDetectMs{}~ms of accumulated detection over their attempts, and \cfResolvedOverheadMs{}~ms of transport overhead. The detection cost is architectural rather than algorithmic: as documented in Section~\ref{sec:conflict_detection}, every check re-fetches the candidate and all referenced peer trajectories (up to 99 dense trajectories of roughly 1700 samples each, late in the run) from the trajectory store and rebuilds the detector state in a fresh process. This is why the end-to-end time of conflict-checked requests exceeds the planner-stage subtotal of Section~\ref{sec:planner_scalability} by more than an order of magnitude, and it identifies peer re-fetching and detector-state rebuilding, not trajectory generation, as the dominant cost of the conflict-checked path.

\paragraph{Traffic-density scenarios.}
Table~\ref{tab:conflict_density} repeats the identical request sequence with the schedule-time window compressed from the full day to 60, 30, and 15 minutes. Conflict frequency rises monotonically with density, and the dense windows expose the outcome classes that the sparse baseline cannot: retry-limit exhaustion and no-route outcomes, in which lateral re-routing runs out of alternatives because every remaining route is blocked or the request-local graph becomes disconnected at the origin. Mapping stalls did not occur in any run. Conditional on having at least one temporally overlapping peer, the initial-conflict probability grows from \cfConflictRate{}\% (full day) to \cfFifteenRate{}\% (15-minute window); even in the densest window the number of temporally overlapping pairs exceeds the number of reported conflicts by a wide margin, because overlap in time only selects the pairs that are examined co-temporally. Median accepted detours for resolved requests remain moderate (between \cfDenseDetourMedianMin{} and \cfDenseDetourMedianMax{} in the dense windows), with individual worst cases up to a detour ratio of \cfDenseDetourMax{}.

\paragraph{Deterministic encounter cases.}
Table~\ref{tab:deterministic_cases} lists the five scripted encounter geometries and their observed outcomes; all match the expected behavior. Opposite-direction and timed node-crossing encounters are detected and resolved laterally, and the crossing conflict is confirmed to lie within \SI{556}{m} of the shared graph node---a check that exercises the corrected closest-approach time directly, since the conflict is located by mapping that time onto the candidate trajectory. A same-route follower \SI{15}{s} in trail conflicts along every lateral alternative and ends with an exhausted request-local graph (no route). The two shared-destination cases likewise terminate with no route left: because the conflict now maps to the arrival edge into the shared pad rather than to the departure edge, the alternatives into that pad are exhausted within three attempts instead of consuming the full retry budget. These cases delimit what the implemented lateral-only strategy can and cannot resolve.
