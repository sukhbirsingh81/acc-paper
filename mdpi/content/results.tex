\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

We report the results in two parts, one per experiment of Section~\ref{sec:evaluation_methodology}: the planner-stage latency with deconfliction switched off (RQ1), and the runs with conflict checking switched on (RQ2 and RQ3). Appendix~\ref{sec:blan_wohl_trajectory} shows one example request from start to end---route, waypoints, altitude and speed---for readers who want to see what the pipeline produces before looking at the numbers.

\subsection{Planner-Stage Latency Decomposition and Route-Length Dependence}
\label{sec:planner_scalability}

This experiment tests the Planning Service alone, without the Deconfliction Service. It is a sequential baseline over one fixed batch of requests and shows how the latency depends on path length on one fixed network; it does not test concurrent load or larger networks. \paPairs{} random origin--destination pairs were processed one after another, each pair \paReps{} times after five warmup requests, which gives \paObs{} timing observations over \paPairs{} different routes. All requests used the Hamburg corridor graph and the fixed \SI{200}{m} MSL cruise layer. All requests succeeded, so no failed cases were removed. As explained in Section~\ref{sec:evaluation_methodology}, the runtime distributions below use all \paObs{} observations, and all regressions, confidence intervals and correlations use the \paPairs{} per-pair means.

Table~\ref{tab:planner_scalability_runtime} shows the runtime of the planner stages. We call the sum of A* search, waypoint synthesis, and CFMS invocation and dense trajectory generation the \emph{planner-stage subtotal}. Its mean is \SI{\paPlannerMean}{ms}, its \(p_{90}\) is \SI{\paPlannerPninety}{ms}, and its maximum is \SI{\paPlannerMax}{ms} over the \paObs{} observations. This is fast enough for interactive planning and quick route previews in our local setup, but it says nothing about operation under distributed or concurrent traffic. Most of this time is CFMS invocation and dense trajectory generation, with a mean of \SI{\paGenMean}{ms}. Waypoint synthesis and A* search both stay below one millisecond, with maxima of \SI{\paWpMax}{ms} and \SI{\paAstarMax}{ms}.

So trajectory generation takes almost all of the planning time, and A* and waypoint synthesis hardly matter. Based on the mean values in Table~\ref{tab:planner_scalability_runtime}, it makes up about \paGenShare{}\% of the planner-stage subtotal. Because of this, the subtotal depends on route length in the same way as trajectory generation does.

Figure~\ref{fig:planner_scalability_distance} plots the planner-stage times over path distance, one point per OD pair. The subtotal and the trajectory-generation time both grow clearly with distance (Pearson \(r = \paPlannerDistR{}\) and \(r = \paGenDistR{}\)). A linear regression of the subtotal on path distance gives a slope of \SI{\paPlannerDistSlope}{ms/km} (95\% confidence interval \([\paPlannerDistLo{}, \paPlannerDistHi{}]\), \(R^2 = \paPlannerDistRsq{}\), \(n = \paPairs{}\) OD pairs). Two more regressions explain why. The number of trajectory samples grows almost exactly linearly with distance (\paSamplesDistSlope{} samples per km, 95\% CI \([\paSamplesDistLo{}, \paSamplesDistHi{}]\), \(R^2 = \paSamplesDistRsq{}\)), and the trajectory-generation time grows linearly with the number of samples (\SI{\paGenSamplesSlope}{ms} per sample, 95\% CI \([\paGenSamplesLo{}, \paGenSamplesHi{}]\), \(R^2 = \paGenSamplesRsq{}\)). Longer routes take more time mainly because they produce more trajectory samples. Waypoint synthesis (Pearson \(r = \paWpDistR{}\)) and A* search (Pearson \(r = \paAstarDistR{}\)) stay small over the whole distance range. So on this network, route search is not the bottleneck.

\subsection{Conflict-Resolution-Enabled Evaluation}
\label{sec:conflict_resolution_results}

A second sequential batch of \cfRequests{} requests was run with conflict checking and lateral re-routing switched on, using the detector configuration and closest-approach reporting of Section~\ref{sec:conflict_detection}. Every accepted trajectory was kept as a peer for the later requests. For each new request, the Planning Service first generated a candidate trajectory and then sent it to the Deconfliction Service. Any conflicts were mapped to route edges and resolved by lateral re-routing (Section~\ref{sec:conflict_resolution}), until the trajectory was conflict-free or the limit of five attempts was reached.

\paragraph{Full-day baseline.}
All \cfRequests{} requests ended with an accepted, locally deconflicted plan: \cfClear{} were conflict-free at the first check, one---the first request---had no peers to check against, and \cfResolved{} were solved by lateral re-routing. Over the full day, overlap in time between pairs was rare: only \cfOverlapPairs{} of \cfComparisons{} candidate--peer pairs overlapped. Per request it was more common, and \cfWithOverlap{} of the \cfWithPeers{} requests with at least one peer had at least one peer overlapping in time. Real conflicts were still rare: only \cfConflicted{} of these \cfWithOverlap{} requests (\cfConflictRate{}\%) had a conflict at the first check, so even when two flights are in the air at the same time, they are usually far enough apart. The \cfResolved{} resolved requests needed \cfAttemptList{} planning attempts, blocked \cfBlockedEdges{} directed edges in total, and had detour ratios of \cfDetourList{} compared with their first shortest route. No request hit the retry limit, and there were no mapping stalls or routing errors. With only \cfResolved{} conflicts, this run shows that the loop works, but it is not a statistical estimate of how well it resolves conflicts. Figure~\ref{fig:conflict_resolution_eval} shows the outcomes and attempt counts, and Table~\ref{tab:conflict_resolution_cases} lists every conflict case.

\paragraph{End-to-end latency decomposition.}
Figure~\ref{fig:conflict_latency_decomposition} splits the total request time seen by the client into planning (including storing the results), conflict detection, and HTTP/serialization overhead, separately for requests that were conflict-free at the first check and for resolved requests. Conflict-free requests take \cfClearClientMs{}~ms on average, of which only \cfClearPlanMs{}~ms is planning and \cfClearOverheadMs{}~ms is transport overhead; the other \cfClearDetectMs{}~ms is the single conflict-detection call. Resolved requests take \cfResolvedClientMs{}~ms on average, with \cfResolvedPlanMs{}~ms planning, \cfResolvedDetectMs{}~ms detection summed over all attempts, and \cfResolvedOverheadMs{}~ms overhead. The detection cost comes from the architecture and not from the detection algorithm: as described in Section~\ref{sec:conflict_detection}, every check loads the candidate and all peer trajectories again---up to 99 at the end of the run, each with around one thousand samples---and rebuilds the detector state. That is why a conflict-checked request takes more than ten times longer than the planner-stage subtotal of Section~\ref{sec:planner_scalability}, and why loading peers and rebuilding the detector state, and not trajectory generation, is the main cost here. The growth with the peer set can be seen inside the run. We compare the first and the last quarter of the requests that were conflict-free at the first check, in the order they arrived; both groups make exactly one detection call, so their means can be compared directly. Detection goes up from \cfClearDetectFirstMs{}~ms with \cfClearDetectFirstPeersLo{}--\cfClearDetectFirstPeersHi{} peers to \cfClearDetectLastMs{}~ms with \cfClearDetectLastPeersLo{}--\cfClearDetectLastPeersHi{} peers, about seven times more (\(n = \cfClearDetectFirstN{}\) requests per group). So the run means hide a cost that is still growing at the end of the sequence.

\paragraph{Traffic-density scenarios.}
Table~\ref{tab:conflict_density} repeats the same request sequence with the scheduling window reduced from the full day to 60, 30 and 15 minutes. All four runs use the same OD sequence, order and schedule-time seed, so they compare one traffic sample with itself under a shorter time window; they are not samples of a traffic population. The number of conflicts goes up as the window gets shorter, and the dense runs show outcomes that the full-day run does not have: requests that hit the retry limit, and requests with no route left, where re-routing runs out of alternatives because all remaining routes are blocked or the origin has no free edge anymore. Mapping stalls did not happen in any run. Since only conflict-free requests become peers, the rejected requests also make the peer set smaller: over the full day all \cfRequests{} plans are kept, but only \cfSixtyRetained{}, \cfThirtyRetained{} and \cfDenseRetainedMin{} in the 60, 30 and 15 minute windows. The conflicts in the dense runs are therefore counted against a peer set that is itself thinned out by the density, which slows down the growth of both conflicts and detection cost. In this one fixed-seed run, the share of requests with a first-check conflict among the requests with at least one overlapping peer went up from \cfConflictRate{}\% (full day) to \cfFifteenRate{}\% (15 minutes). These shares describe this run only and are not conflict probabilities in general. Even in the densest window, many more pairs overlap in time than there are conflicts, because overlap in time only decides which pairs are checked. The median detour of resolved requests stays moderate (between \cfDenseDetourMedianMin{} and \cfDenseDetourMedianMax{} in the dense windows), and the longest accepted detour has a ratio of \cfDenseDetourMax{}. Requests that were given up at the retry limit had even longer routes---up to \cfDenseDetourAbandonedMax{} times their first shortest route in the 15-minute window---but these routes were never accepted, so we report them separately. The largest ratios happen on short first routes, where one blocked edge forces a detour around a large part of the network: the \cfDenseDetourAbandonedMax{} case starts from a \SI{\cfDenseDetourAbandonedMaxInitialKm}{km} direct connection.

\paragraph{Deterministic encounter cases.}
Table~\ref{tab:deterministic_cases} lists the five scripted encounters and their outcomes; all behave as expected. Head-on and node-crossing encounters are detected and solved laterally. For the crossing case, the conflict lies within \SI{556}{m} of the shared node, which also tests the reported closest-approach time, because the conflict is located by mapping this time onto the candidate trajectory. A follower \SI{15}{s} behind on the same route is in conflict on every lateral alternative and ends with no route left. The two cases with a shared destination also end with no route left: the conflict maps to the last edge into the shared pad, and all alternatives into that pad are used up within three attempts. These cases show what lateral-only re-routing can and cannot solve.
