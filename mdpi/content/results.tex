\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{BLAN--WOHL Trajectory Case}
\label{sec:blan_wohl_trajectory}

This section first presents one representative trajectory case to illustrate the output of the planner pipeline, followed by the runtime and conflict-resolution results for the benchmark requests. To illustrate the route and trajectory-generation result produced by the Planning Service, we use the request with take-off vertiport \texttt{BLAN} and landing vertiport \texttt{WOHL}. The request uses the fixed cruise layer of \SI{200}{m} MSL and does not invoke the Deconfliction Service; deconfliction is treated as a separate service boundary in this architecture. This case was selected because it traverses multiple corridor segments and clearly separates departure, cruise, and arrival phases. For this origin--destination pair, the A* planner selects the corridor sequence
\[
\texttt{BLAN} \rightarrow \texttt{VOLK} \rightarrow \texttt{SCHN} \rightarrow
\texttt{FUHB} \rightarrow \texttt{WOHL}.
\]

Figure~\ref{fig:blan_wohl_paths_overview} shows this route in the context of the Hamburg corridor graph. The thinner blue edges represent available corridor connections, the red markers show graph nodes, and the thicker blue polyline highlights the selected A* path from \texttt{BLAN} to \texttt{WOHL}. This overview makes the global routing result visible before the route is expanded into a detailed 4D trajectory.

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{figures/blan_wohl_paths_overview.png}
  \caption{A* corridor path overview for the \texttt{BLAN} to \texttt{WOHL} request. The highlighted path connects the departure vertiport \texttt{BLAN} with the landing vertiport \texttt{WOHL} through the selected corridor sequence via the intermediate vertiports \texttt{VOLK}, \texttt{SCHN}, and \texttt{FUHB}.}
  \label{fig:blan_wohl_paths_overview}
\end{figure}

The waypoint synthesizer expands this five-node route into 11 CFMS input waypoints, including departure, transition, cruise, top-of-descent, and landing waypoints. The three internal cruise-node waypoints corresponding to \texttt{VOLK}, \texttt{SCHN}, and \texttt{FUHB} are retained as centerline route references for CFMS input. The resulting dense trajectory contains 1713 samples, covers a cumulative distance of \SI{27.99}{km}, and has a total duration of \SI{1596.74}{s}, or \SI{26.61}{min}. The mean true airspeed over the generated trajectory is \SI{17.15}{m/s}, with the cruise segment reaching approximately \SI{25.00}{m/s}.

Figure~\ref{fig:blan_wohl_map} separates the lateral trajectory by the main planning phases. The departure panel shows the aircraft leaving \texttt{BLAN}, climbing through the terminal waypoints, and joining the corridor at \texttt{VOLK}. The initial climb is flown essentially vertically over the departure pad, so the lateral track leaves \texttt{BLAN} directly along the outbound corridor direction without a low-height turning maneuver around the pad. The cruise panel shows the route through the selected corridor nodes \texttt{VOLK}, \texttt{SCHN}, and \texttt{FUHB}; the landing panel shows the inbound transition from \texttt{FUHB} toward the destination vertiport \texttt{WOHL}, which ends in a vertical final descent over the destination pad. The figure therefore confirms that the generated trajectory follows the A*-selected corridor sequence while adding the terminal geometry required at both endpoints. The corresponding vertical and speed profiles are shown in Figs.~\ref{fig:blan_wohl_altitude} and~\ref{fig:blan_wohl_tas}.

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.92\linewidth]{figures/blan_wohl_trajectory_map.png}
  \caption{Phase-separated lateral trajectory for the \texttt{BLAN} to \texttt{WOHL} request. Red markers indicate synthesized CFMS waypoints; the blue line is the generated trajectory. The panels separate the take-off and ascent portion, the corridor cruise portion, and the final landing transition. Boundary waypoints are repeated across adjacent panels to show phase continuity.}
  \label{fig:blan_wohl_map}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:blan_wohl_altitude} shows the vertical profile of the generated trajectory. The three panels use local phase-relative time axes rather than one continuous global time axis, so each phase can be inspected at an appropriate temporal scale. During take-off and ascent, the trajectory first climbs vertically from the pad through the lower terminal height and then climbs steeply along the outbound leg to the outer-limitation-surface reference at \SI{152}{m}, which it holds until reaching the first corridor node below the cruise layer. The aircraft then reaches and maintains the \SI{200}{m} MSL cruise layer during the central corridor segment. On arrival, the trajectory descends from the cruise layer to the \SI{152}{m} approach reference shortly after leaving the last corridor segment, holds this level on the inbound leg until the top of descent, and then descends through the \SI{60}{m} and \SI{15}{m} terminal references before a vertical final descent onto the \texttt{WOHL} pad. This profile illustrates how the waypoint-synthesis logic combines the fixed cruise altitude with structured vertiport departure and arrival procedures.

The three panels show take-off/ascent, cruise, and landing using local phase-relative time axes rather than a single global time axis. The profile reaches the fixed \SI{200}{m} MSL cruise layer before descending into \texttt{WOHL}.}

Figure~\ref{fig:blan_wohl_tas} shows the corresponding true airspeed profile. As in the altitude profile, the panels use local phase-relative time axes rather than one global time axis. After the vertical lift-off, the aircraft flies the departure funnel segment at a low constant speed of approximately \SI{5}{m/s}, accelerates to \SI{20}{m/s} on the outbound corridor leg, and reaches the cruise setting of approximately \SI{25}{m/s} along the central corridor segment. On arrival, the speed is reduced in stages through the approach speed limits near the destination vertiport before the final vertical descent at essentially zero horizontal speed. Explicit swept-volume corridor-containment validation remains a downstream check rather than a claim made from this speed profile alone.

\subsection{Planner Computational Scaling Evaluation}
\label{sec:planner_scalability}

The computational scaling experiment evaluates the Planning Service in isolation from the Deconfliction Service. It measures a sequential baseline over a fixed batch of requests and scaling with selected path distance, not concurrent service throughput. A batch of 100 randomly sampled origin--destination requests was processed synchronously. Each request used the Hamburg corridor graph, the fixed \SI{200}{m} MSL cruise layer, and disabled deconfliction. The benchmark pairs were sampled without replacement, each pair had distinct origin and destination vertiports, and no origin--destination pair was repeated. All 100 sampled pairs were connected and all 100 requests completed successfully, so no failed cases were removed before computing the reported statistics.

Table~\ref{tab:planner_scalability_runtime} summarizes the measured runtime distribution for the planner-relevant stages. We use the term \emph{planner-stage subtotal} for the sum of A* path computation, waypoint synthesis, and CFMS invocation with trajectory generation. This subtotal has a mean of \SI{43.72}{ms} and a \(p_{90}\) latency of \SI{59.80}{ms}. This latency range is compatible with interactive planning and rapid route preview in the evaluated local setup, without implying operational readiness under distributed or concurrent traffic conditions. CFMS invocation and trajectory generation dominate this subtotal with a mean of \SI{43.38}{ms}. In contrast, waypoint synthesis and A* path search remain sub-millisecond, with \(p_{99}\) latencies of \SI{0.22}{ms} and \SI{0.40}{ms}, respectively.

CFMS invocation and trajectory generation account for almost the entire planner-stage runtime, while A* and waypoint synthesis are negligible in comparison. Using the mean values in Table~\ref{tab:planner_scalability_runtime}, CFMS invocation and trajectory generation contribute approximately \(43.38/43.72 \approx 99.2\%\) of the planner-stage subtotal. This makes CFMS invocation and trajectory generation the dominant computational stage in the evaluated planner pipeline. Because this stage dominates the subtotal, the distance dependence of the subtotal is expected to follow the distance dependence of the CFMS-related generation stage.

Figure~\ref{fig:planner_scalability_distance} relates the planner-stage runtimes to selected path distance. The planner-stage subtotal and CFMS invocation with trajectory generation both increase strongly with distance (Pearson \(r=0.97\)), showing that route length primarily affects the downstream trajectory-generation workload. The likely reason is that longer corridor routes produce longer dense trajectories, more trajectory samples, and therefore larger CFMS generation workloads. Waypoint synthesis (Pearson \(r=0.08\)) and A* path search (Pearson \(r=0.20\)) show little distance sensitivity and remain small in absolute terms. This indicates that, for the evaluated network, route search is not the limiting factor.

\subsection{Conflict-Resolution-Enabled Evaluation}
\label{sec:conflict_resolution_results}

A second sequential batch of 100 trajectory requests was evaluated with conflict checking and lateral re-routing enabled. In this run, every locally deconflicted trajectory was retained as a peer trajectory for later requests. For each new request, the Planning Service first generated a candidate 4D trajectory and then submitted it to the Deconfliction Service. If NDMap reported conflicts, the Planning Service mapped the reported conflict time to the closest point on the candidate trajectory, identified the corresponding corridor-graph edge, removed that edge for the current request only, and recomputed the trajectory. This process was repeated until the trajectory was conflict-free or the configured retry limit was reached.

All 100 submitted candidate-planning requests were retained as locally deconflicted plans. Of these, 93 were conflict-free on the first deconfliction check and one was the first request in the sequence, for which no peer trajectory was available. Six requests were successfully rerouted after conflicts were detected. Across the full run, six requests had an initial conflict and the Planning Service removed 13 request-local directed graph edges in total; the resolved requests required between two and four planning attempts. Because only six requests encountered initial conflicts, these results should be interpreted as preliminary evidence that the edge-removal feedback loop can recover from some conflicts without modifying the persistent corridor graph, not as a statistical estimate of conflict-resolution performance or airspace capacity.

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
  \centering
  \subfloat[Final request outcomes]{\includegraphics[width=0.48\textwidth]{figures/conflict_resolution_status_counts.png}}
  \hfill
  \subfloat[Planning attempts for checked requests]{\includegraphics[width=0.48\textwidth]{figures/conflict_resolution_attempts.png}}
  \caption{Conflict-resolution-enabled evaluation over 100 sequential trajectory requests. The status plot separates conflict-free, resolved, and first-request unchecked outcomes; the attempts plot shows how many planning attempts were required when conflict checking was performed.}
  \label{fig:conflict_resolution_eval}
\end{figure}

The successful conflict-free requests had a mean end-to-end request time of \SI{63.51}{ms} and a median of \SI{60.98}{ms}. The six resolved requests were substantially slower, with a mean end-to-end request time of \SI{3947.19}{ms}. This increase is expected because each conflict-resolution attempt regenerates a full trajectory and repeats deconfliction against the retained peer-trajectory set. The mean planner-stage subtotal for the resolved requests was \SI{225.56}{ms}, compared with \SI{49.78}{ms} for conflict-free checked requests.

No request in this run failed: every initial conflict was resolved by lateral re-routing within the configured retry limit of five attempts, and no routing or re-planning error occurred. The evaluation therefore contains no retry-limit or routing-error outcomes to analyze; denser traffic scenarios would be required to probe the failure modes of lateral-only re-routing.
