\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{BLAN--WOHL Trajectory Case}
\label{sec:blan_wohl_trajectory}

This section first presents one representative trajectory case to illustrate the output of the planner pipeline, followed by the runtime and conflict-resolution results for the benchmark requests. To illustrate the route and trajectory-generation result produced by the Planning Service, we use the request with take-off vertiport \texttt{BLAN} and landing vertiport \texttt{WOHL}. The request uses the fixed cruise layer of \SI{200}{m} MSL and does not invoke the Deconfliction Service; deconfliction is treated as a separate service boundary in this architecture. This case was selected because it traverses multiple corridor segments and clearly separates departure, cruise, and arrival phases. For this origin--destination pair, the A* planner selects the corridor sequence
\[
\texttt{BLAN} \rightarrow \texttt{VOLK} \rightarrow \texttt{SCHN} \rightarrow
\texttt{FUHB} \rightarrow \texttt{WOHL}.
\]

Figure~\ref{fig:blan_wohl_paths_overview} shows this route in the context of the Hamburg corridor graph. The thinner blue edges represent available corridor connections, the red markers show graph nodes, and the thicker blue polyline highlights the selected A* path from \texttt{BLAN} to \texttt{WOHL}. This overview makes the global routing result visible before the route is expanded into a detailed 4D trajectory.

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{figures/blan_wohl_paths_overview.png}
  \caption{A* corridor path overview for the \texttt{BLAN} to \texttt{WOHL} request. The highlighted path connects the departure vertiport \texttt{BLAN} with the landing vertiport \texttt{WOHL} through the selected corridor sequence via the intermediate vertiports \texttt{VOLK}, \texttt{SCHN}, and \texttt{FUHB}.}
  \label{fig:blan_wohl_paths_overview}
\end{figure}

The waypoint synthesizer expands this five-node route into 12 CFMS input waypoints, including departure, transition, cruise, top-of-descent, and landing waypoints. The three internal cruise-node waypoints corresponding to \texttt{VOLK}, \texttt{SCHN}, and \texttt{FUHB} are retained as centerline route references for CFMS input. The resulting dense trajectory contains 1892 samples, covers a cumulative distance of \SI{29.49}{km}, and has a total duration of \SI{1558.59}{s}, or \SI{25.98}{min}. The mean true airspeed over the generated trajectory is \SI{17.07}{m/s}, with the cruise segment reaching approximately \SI{25.00}{m/s}.

Figure~\ref{fig:blan_wohl_map} separates the lateral trajectory by the main planning phases. The departure panel shows the aircraft leaving \texttt{BLAN}, climbing through the terminal waypoints, and joining the corridor at \texttt{VOLK}. A small localized loop is visible in the lateral track near the origin pad at waypoint~1. This loop is generated by CFMS during the transition from the vertical climb segment to the outbound corridor direction: because the synthesized departure waypoints are nearly co-located laterally while requiring a heading alignment toward the corridor, CFMS produces a short turning maneuver at low speed around the pad. The maneuver is interpreted as trajectory-generator behavior for the synthesized waypoint sequence rather than as a result of the graph-routing stage. The cruise panel shows the route through the selected corridor nodes \texttt{VOLK}, \texttt{SCHN}, and \texttt{FUHB}; the landing panel shows the inbound transition from \texttt{FUHB} toward the destination vertiport \texttt{WOHL}. The figure therefore confirms that the generated trajectory follows the A*-selected corridor sequence while adding the terminal geometry required at both endpoints. The corresponding vertical and speed profiles are shown in Figs.~\ref{fig:blan_wohl_altitude} and~\ref{fig:blan_wohl_tas}.

The panels separate the take-off and ascent portion, the corridor cruise portion, and the final landing transition. Boundary waypoints are repeated across adjacent panels to show phase continuity.}
  \label{fig:blan_wohl_map}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:blan_wohl_altitude} shows the vertical profile of the generated trajectory. The three panels use local phase-relative time axes rather than one continuous global time axis, so each phase can be inspected at an appropriate temporal scale. During take-off and ascent, the trajectory climbs from the pad through the terminal heights and reaches the first corridor node below the cruise layer. The aircraft then reaches and maintains the \SI{200}{m} MSL cruise layer during the central corridor segment. On arrival, the trajectory holds the cruise layer until the inbound descent segment, then descends through the \SI{60}{m} and \SI{15}{m} terminal references before reaching the \texttt{WOHL} pad. This profile illustrates how the waypoint-synthesis logic combines the fixed cruise altitude with structured vertiport departure and arrival procedures.

The three panels show take-off/ascent, cruise, and landing using local phase-relative time axes rather than a single global time axis. The profile reaches the fixed \SI{200}{m} MSL cruise layer before descending into \texttt{WOHL}.}
  \label{fig:blan_wohl_altitude}

Figure~\ref{fig:blan_wohl_tas} shows the corresponding true airspeed profile. As in the altitude profile, the panels use local phase-relative time axes rather than one global time axis. The aircraft accelerates from the terminal take-off segment into the outbound transition, reaches the higher cruise setting along the central corridor segment, and then decelerates for the arrival and landing procedure. The cruise panel also shows localized speed reductions near internal corridor nodes. These reductions are consistent with CFMS-generated transitions between adjacent A*-selected route legs before acceleration on the following corridor segment. Explicit swept-volume corridor-containment validation remains a downstream check rather than a claim made from this speed profile alone.

\subsection{Planner Computational Scaling Evaluation}
\label{sec:planner_scalability}

The computational scaling experiment evaluates the Planning Service in isolation from the Deconfliction Service. It measures a sequential baseline over a fixed batch of requests and scaling with selected path distance, not concurrent service throughput. A batch of 100 randomly sampled origin--destination requests was processed synchronously. Each request used the Hamburg corridor graph, the fixed \SI{200}{m} MSL cruise layer, and disabled deconfliction. The benchmark pairs were sampled without replacement, each pair had distinct origin and destination vertiports, and no origin--destination pair was repeated. All 100 sampled pairs were connected and all 100 requests completed successfully, so no failed cases were removed before computing the reported statistics.

Table~\ref{tab:planner_scalability_runtime} summarizes the measured runtime distribution for the planner-relevant stages. We use the term \emph{planner-stage subtotal} for the sum of A* path computation, waypoint synthesis, and CFMS invocation with trajectory generation. This subtotal has a mean of \SI{88.94}{ms} and a \(p_{90}\) latency of \SI{132.24}{ms}. This latency range is compatible with interactive planning and rapid route preview in the evaluated local setup, without implying operational readiness under distributed or concurrent traffic conditions. CFMS invocation and trajectory generation dominate this subtotal with a mean of \SI{88.27}{ms}. In contrast, waypoint synthesis and A* path search remain sub-millisecond, with \(p_{99}\) latencies of \SI{0.41}{ms} and \SI{0.83}{ms}, respectively.

CFMS invocation and trajectory generation account for almost the entire planner-stage runtime, while A* and waypoint synthesis are negligible in comparison. Using the mean values in Table~\ref{tab:planner_scalability_runtime}, CFMS invocation and trajectory generation contribute approximately \(88.27/88.94 \approx 99.2\%\) of the planner-stage subtotal. This makes CFMS invocation and trajectory generation the dominant computational stage in the evaluated planner pipeline. Because this stage dominates the subtotal, the distance dependence of the subtotal is expected to follow the distance dependence of the CFMS-related generation stage.

Figure~\ref{fig:planner_scalability_distance} relates the planner-stage runtimes to selected path distance. The planner-stage subtotal and CFMS invocation with trajectory generation both increase strongly with distance (Pearson \(r=0.90\)), showing that route length primarily affects the downstream trajectory-generation workload. The likely reason is that longer corridor routes produce longer dense trajectories, more trajectory samples, and therefore larger CFMS generation workloads. Waypoint synthesis (Pearson \(r=-0.06\)) and A* path search (Pearson \(r=0.11\)) show little distance sensitivity and remain small in absolute terms. This indicates that, for the evaluated network, route search is not the limiting factor.

\subsection{Conflict-Resolution-Enabled Evaluation}
\label{sec:conflict_resolution_results}

A second sequential batch of 100 trajectory requests was evaluated with conflict checking and lateral re-routing enabled. In this run, every locally deconflicted trajectory was retained as a peer trajectory for later requests. For each new request, the Planning Service first generated a candidate 4D trajectory and then submitted it to the Deconfliction Service. If NDMap reported conflicts, the Planning Service mapped the reported conflict time to the closest point on the candidate trajectory, identified the corresponding corridor-graph edge, removed that edge for the current request only, and recomputed the trajectory. This process was repeated until the trajectory was conflict-free or the configured retry limit was reached.

Out of the 100 submitted candidate-planning requests, 97 were retained as locally deconflicted plans. Of these retained requests, 93 were conflict-free on the first deconfliction check and one was the first request in the sequence, for which no peer trajectory was available. Three requests were successfully rerouted after conflicts were detected. Across the full run, four requests had an initial conflict and the Planning Service removed ten request-local directed graph edges in total. Because only four requests encountered initial conflicts, these results should be interpreted as preliminary evidence that the edge-removal feedback loop can recover from some conflicts without modifying the persistent corridor graph, not as a statistical estimate of conflict-resolution performance or airspace capacity.

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
  \centering
  \subfloat[Final request outcomes]{\includegraphics[width=0.48\textwidth]{figures/conflict_resolution_status_counts.png}}
  \hfill
  \subfloat[Planning attempts for checked requests]{\includegraphics[width=0.48\textwidth]{figures/conflict_resolution_attempts.png}}
  \caption{Conflict-resolution-enabled evaluation over 100 sequential trajectory requests. The status plot separates conflict-free, resolved, retry-limit, and routing-error outcomes; the attempts plot shows how many planning attempts were required when conflict checking was performed.}
  \label{fig:conflict_resolution_eval}
\end{figure}

The successful conflict-free requests had a mean end-to-end request time of \SI{152.96}{ms} and a median of \SI{148.06}{ms}. The three resolved requests were substantially slower, with a mean end-to-end request time of \SI{3130.58}{ms}. This increase is expected because each conflict-resolution attempt regenerates a full trajectory and repeats deconfliction against the retained peer-trajectory set. The mean planner-stage subtotal for the resolved requests was \SI{490.16}{ms}, compared with \SI{129.49}{ms} for conflict-free checked requests.

Table~\ref{tab:conflict_resolution_failures} summarizes the three failed cases in the CSV. One failure was a genuine lateral-rerouting failure: request \texttt{HCTY}\(\rightarrow\)\texttt{HARB} detected an initial conflict, removed five outgoing \texttt{HCTY} edges over five attempts, and still reached the configured retry limit. The other two failures returned HTTP~400 with the route builder reporting that the current start node had no outgoing edges. Inspection of the saved artifacts indicates that candidate paths were generated for these OD pairs, so these rows are classified as prototype routing/input-graph or re-planning integration failures rather than unresolved NDMap conflicts.
