\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The planner-stage experiment shows that the evaluated service decomposition is well aligned with the computational structure of the path-planning problem. A* path search and waypoint synthesis contribute only a small fraction of the planner-stage runtime, whereas CFMS invocation and dense trajectory generation dominate the measured cost and its route-length dependence. For the evaluated Hamburg corridor network, graph-based routing is therefore not the main computational bottleneck; the limiting stage is converting a selected corridor route into a dense, time-parameterized 4D trajectory. The regression analysis supports the mechanism behind this dependence: the trajectory sample count grows almost perfectly linearly with route length, and the trajectory-generation time grows linearly with the sample count, so longer routes cost more primarily because they produce proportionally more trajectory samples.

This finding is relevant for route updates and contingency planning. If a corridor edge must be removed because of a closure, geofence, or conflict-driven lateral re-routing step, the low A* runtime suggests that alternative lateral routes can be found quickly. The larger delay arises from regenerating the corresponding 4D trajectory and, when conflict checking is enabled, from repeating conflict detection against the retained peer set.

The conflict-resolution-enabled evaluation, rerun after correcting the vertical-separation unit conversion and the closest-approach reporting in the detector, demonstrates the NDMap and lateral re-routing loop under both sparse and dense scheduling. Under the full-day baseline, temporal overlap between requests is rare and most candidates are conflict-free on the first check, with a small number of conflicts resolved by request-local edge removal. Compressing the scheduling window increases temporal overlap and conflict frequency substantially and exposes the outcomes that the sparse baseline cannot probe: retry-limit cases and exhaustion of lateral alternatives. No mapping stall occurred in any run, so that outcome class remains implemented and instrumented but unobserved. The deterministic encounter suite confirms the intended geometry-level behavior: opposite-direction and crossing encounters are detected and resolved laterally, whereas same-pad merges at a shared landing time and dense in-trail traffic on a shared route cannot be resolved by lateral re-routing alone. These outcomes are pre-submission planning and deconfliction results, not USSP authorization decisions.

The temporal semantics of the detector deserve explicit emphasis, because interval overlap and co-temporal proximity are easily conflated. Two candidate trajectories that share a scheduling window, or even an entire flight interval, are not in conflict on that account: the detector requires a single instant at which both interpolated positions violate the horizontal and vertical minima simultaneously. This is visible in the full-day baseline, where most requests have at least one temporally overlapping peer yet only a few of them report a conflict, and it remains true in the compressed windows, where temporally overlapping pairs continue to outnumber conflicting requests by more than an order of magnitude. Correcting the closest-approach reporting also changed which edge a conflict removes: previously every conflict was mapped to the first sample of the candidate trajectory and therefore blocked the departure edge, whereas the corrected records map each conflict to the route edge where the encounter actually occurs.

The end-to-end latency decomposition answers where the time goes when conflict checking is enabled. The planner stage itself remains tens of milliseconds, but each conflict check re-fetches the candidate and all referenced peer trajectories from the trajectory store, rebuilds the detector state in a fresh process, and only then evaluates the conflict predicate. This per-check cost grows with the accumulating peer set and dominates end-to-end request time long before trajectory generation does; resolved requests additionally multiply both costs by the number of attempts. The stateless-detector design was chosen for state consistency, and the measured cost is its price.

The main optimization targets are therefore persistent or incrementally updated detector state, so that unchanged peer trajectories are not re-fetched and re-indexed on every check, followed by trajectory-store caching and clearer separation between visualization-oriented outputs and conflict-checking inputs. More capable resolution strategies should also include temporal and vertical deconfliction, because the evaluated encounters show concretely that lateral re-routing alone cannot resolve shared-destination or dense in-trail conflicts and can exhaust the available graph alternatives.

The corridor-based airspace assumption underlying this work also deserves critical reflection. The U-space framework does not prescribe or mandate a corridor topology; U-space services are provided within designated regions or volumes in which operators can plan their trajectories freely, and corridor structures are only one option envisioned by some operators for early UAM implementation, as investigated, for example, in the SESAR project EUREKA~\cite{sesar_eureka_project}. Corridors were selected for this study because they map directly onto a graph representation, simplify procedural design and conflict geometry, and reflect structures plausible for initial operations. However, comparative airspace studies indicate that more structured airspace generally trades capacity and routing flexibility for predictability: layered or less constrained concepts can achieve higher throughput at high traffic densities~\cite{sunil2015metropolis,schneider2014metropolis,abdellaoui2025_airspace_uam,schuchardt2023_atm_uam_review}. This trade-off is structural for the evaluated approach, since a corridor graph offers only a finite set of lateral alternatives to the re-routing loop---a limit the dense-window and deterministic scenarios reach in practice. The presented architecture does not depend on the corridor assumption itself---the fleet webserver's topology provision could equally serve other route-network abstractions---but the reported capacity-related behavior is specific to the evaluated corridor model.

The evaluation remains limited. Requests are processed sequentially, the services run locally on one machine, the graph is small, the cruise layer is fixed at \SI{200}{m} MSL, and the scenario excludes terrain, weather, obstacle avoidance, no-fly zones, dynamic traffic demand, and concurrent service load. Within these bounds, the results characterize the computational behavior of the implemented pipeline; they do not constitute an operational capacity assessment.
