\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The planner experiment identifies where computation time is spent within the Planning Service. A* search and waypoint synthesis take only a small part of the planning time, while CFMS invocation and dense trajectory generation take most of it and account for the observed association with route length. On the Hamburg network, graph routing is therefore not the main bottleneck; the slow part is turning a route into a dense 4D trajectory. The regressions support this interpretation: the number of trajectory samples grows almost linearly with route length, and trajectory-generation time grows linearly with the number of samples, consistent with sample count contributing to the runtime increase. This is an association within the benchmark, not a controlled test of sampling cost.

This matters for route updates and contingency planning. If a corridor edge has to be removed because of a closure, a geofence or a conflict, the short A* runtime means that a new lateral route can be found quickly. The larger delay comes from generating the new 4D trajectory and, with conflict checking, from checking it again against all stored peers.

The conflict experiment shows the NDMap and re-routing loop working with both sparse and dense traffic. Over the full day, no conflict was detected for most candidates at the first check, and the few detected conflicts were resolved by request-local edge removal. For the evaluated request sequence, a shorter scheduling window leads to more temporal overlap and clearly more detected conflicts, and we see outcomes that the sparse run cannot show: requests hitting the retry limit and requests running out of lateral alternatives. Mapping stalls did not occur in any run, so this outcome is implemented and logged but was never observed. The scripted encounters illustrate both outcomes: the head-on and crossing requests are resolved, while the following and shared-destination requests exhaust the reduced graph. These are outcomes of this heuristic; graph exhaustion does not prove that every lateral alternative in the original network is infeasible. All these outcomes are results of planning and deconfliction before submission, not USSP authorization decisions.

The temporal semantics of the detector require care, because temporal overlap and a detected conflict are easily conflated. Two trajectories that share a scheduling window, or even their whole flight time, are not in conflict because of that. The detector needs one moment at which both positions break the horizontal and vertical minima at the same time. Per pair, overlap is rare in the full-day run (\cfOverlapPairs{} of \cfComparisons{} pairs). Per request it is more common, because a request has many peers and needs only one of them to overlap; \cfWithOverlap{} of \cfWithPeers{} requests have at least one overlapping peer, but only \cfConflicted{} of them have a conflict. The same holds in the shorter windows. Conflict and overlap counts must therefore be read together with the unit over which they are counted.

The latency breakdown shows where the time is spent when conflict checking is enabled. Planning stays at tens of milliseconds, but every conflict check reloads the candidate and all peer trajectories from the trajectory store, builds a fresh detector state in a detector process started for that check, and only then runs the check. The measured detection-stage latency grows with the number of peers and becomes much larger than trajectory generation; resolved requests incur it again for every attempt. Because the measured block includes trajectory fetching, detector-state construction and NDMap execution, the present instrumentation does not isolate the intrinsic NDMap query time. The stateless design was chosen for state consistency, and this cost is its price.

The most promising improvement is therefore a detector state that is kept or updated step by step, so that unchanged peer trajectories are not loaded and indexed again for every check. After that come caching in the trajectory store and a clearer split between outputs for visualization and inputs for conflict checking. Resolution should also be extended to time and altitude, because the evaluated heuristic fails on the shared-destination and following cases and can exhaust its reduced graph. The single cruise layer makes this even clearer. With all cruise segments at the same altitude, the vertical criterion is always met between two cruising aircraft, so en-route separation depends only on horizontal distance and lateral re-routing is the only option the loop has. A corridor structure with several layers would make the vertical criterion useful en route and give the loop a second dimension to work with. The conflict numbers in this paper should be read with this single-layer assumption in mind.

The corridor assumption behind this work also deserves critical reflection. U-space does not require corridors. U-space services are provided in a region or volume in which operators plan their trajectories subject to the applicable airspace and operational constraints, and corridors are only one option that some operators want to use for early UAM operations, as studied for example in the SESAR project EUREKA~\cite{sesar_eureka_project}. We chose corridors because they map directly onto a graph, make procedure design and conflict geometry simpler, and are a plausible structure for first operations. On the other hand, airspace studies show that more structure usually means more predictability but less capacity and flexibility: layered or less constrained airspace can handle more traffic at high densities~\cite{sunil2015metropolis,schneider2014metropolis,abdellaoui2025_airspace_uam,schuchardt2023_atm_uam_review}. For our approach this trade-off is built in, since a corridor graph only offers a limited set of lateral alternatives to the re-routing loop---and the dense and scripted cases reach this limit. The architecture itself does not depend on corridors, since the fleet webserver could also provide other kinds of route networks, but the measured conflict and re-routing outcomes are specific to the corridor model we tested.

The limitations of Section~\ref{sec:limitations} apply, and two of them bound the statistical reach of the results. The planner experiment has \paObs{} timing observations but only \paPairs{} distinct OD pairs, so the regressions use \paPairs{} route-level means, subject to the shared-network and regression assumptions stated in Section~\ref{sec:evaluation_methodology}. The density runs use one fixed OD sequence, order and seed; the reported shares therefore belong to this one run and are not general conflict probabilities. Within these limits, the results describe the computational behavior of the prototype pipeline, not the capacity of an operational system.
