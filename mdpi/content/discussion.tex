\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The planner-stage scaling experiment shows that the evaluated service decomposition is well aligned with the computational structure of the path-planning problem. A* path search and waypoint synthesis contribute only a small fraction of the planner-stage runtime, whereas CFMS invocation and trajectory generation dominate the measured cost. For the evaluated Hamburg corridor network, graph-based routing is therefore not the main computational bottleneck; the limiting stage is converting a selected corridor route into a dense, time-parameterized 4D trajectory.

This finding is relevant for route updates and contingency planning. If a corridor edge must be removed because of a closure, geofence, or conflict-driven lateral re-routing step, the low A* runtime suggests that alternative lateral routes can be found quickly. The larger delay arises from regenerating the corresponding 4D trajectory and, when conflict checking is enabled, from repeating deconfliction against the retained peer set.

The conflict-resolution-enabled evaluation demonstrates a preliminary NDMap and lateral re-routing loop rather than only an architectural placeholder. The prototype retained 97 of 100 sequential requests as locally deconflicted candidate plans and resolved three requests after initial conflicts by removing request-local directed graph edges and recomputing the route. These outcomes are not USSP authorisation decisions; they are pre-submission planning and deconfliction results. The resolved cases were substantially slower because each attempt regenerated a full CFMS trajectory and repeated conflict checking. This reinforces the planner-stage finding: lateral alternatives are inexpensive to search for, but repeated 4D trajectory generation is the main cost of closed-loop re-planning.

The failed cases also define the limits of the current prototype. The retry-limit case shows that lateral-only re-routing is not sufficient for all conflicts in a single fixed cruise layer. Some encounters may require temporal shifts, speed adjustments, altitude-layer changes, or a richer search over route alternatives. The two routing/input-graph errors should not be interpreted as unresolved NDMap conflicts; they indicate implementation and integration robustness issues in the request-local re-planning loop. These failures are important for assessing the prototype, but they do not invalidate NDMap conflict detection itself.

The main optimization targets are therefore CFMS process reuse or persistent CFMS workers, trajectory-output management, route and segment caching, and clearer separation between visualization-oriented outputs and conflict-checking inputs. More capable resolution strategies should also include temporal and vertical deconfliction, because lateral re-routing alone can exhaust available graph alternatives or produce longer trajectories without resolving the underlying encounter.

The corridor-based airspace assumption underlying this work also deserves critical reflection. The U-space framework itself does not foresee corridors; U-space services are provided within designated regions or volumes in which operators can plan their trajectories freely, and corridor structures are only one option envisioned by some operators for early UAM implementation, as investigated, for example, in the SESAR project EUREKA~\cite{sesar_eureka_project}. Corridors were selected for this study because they map directly onto a graph representation, simplify procedural design and conflict geometry, and reflect structures plausible for initial operations. However, comparative airspace studies indicate that more structured airspace generally trades capacity and routing flexibility for predictability: layered or less constrained concepts can achieve higher throughput at high traffic densities~\cite{sunil2015metropolis,schneider2014metropolis,abdellaoui2025_airspace_uam,schuchardt2023_atm_uam_review}. The retry-limit failure observed in the conflict-resolution experiment illustrates this trade-off concretely, since a corridor graph offers only a finite set of lateral alternatives. The presented architecture does not depend on the corridor assumption itself --- the Topology Registry could equally serve other route-network abstractions --- but the reported capacity-related behavior is specific to the evaluated corridor model.

The evaluation remains limited. Requests are processed sequentially, the services run locally, the graph is small, the cruise layer is fixed at \SI{200}{m} MSL, and the scenario excludes terrain, weather, obstacle avoidance, no-fly zones, dynamic traffic demand, and concurrent service load. The results should therefore be read as a local baseline for a service-oriented prototype and not as an operational capacity assessment.
