\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{U-space and 4D Trajectory-Based Operations}

The SESAR Master Plan places U-space within the wider move towards trajectory-based operations (TBO) and digital air traffic management~\cite{sesar_master_plan_2025}. The NextGen program of the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) similarly uses negotiated 4D trajectories to improve predictability and capacity~\cite{enea_porretta_4d_tbo_comparison}. Both see time-based trajectory management and interoperable services as key for bringing more air traffic into cities.

Conflict management in discretized 4D space--time has been shown for multi-UAS operations~\cite{acevedo_4d_grids_u_space}. More recent work splits conflict management into a strategic layer, where conflicts are removed before departure by changing the planned traffic, and a tactical layer, which handles the conflicts that remain in flight. Strategic conflict management has been formulated as a collaborative traffic-flow optimization over the operators' mission preferences~\cite{du2025_collaborative_uam_flow}, and resilient detection and resolution methods handle the trajectory uncertainty that a strategic plan alone cannot cover~\cite{badea2025_resilient_cdr}. The check in this paper belongs to the strategic layer. It checks separation against the nominal trajectory without an uncertainty buffer, which is a limitation that the uncertainty-aware work points out~\cite{badea2025_resilient_cdr}. U-space service architectures have also been proposed that combine monitoring, traffic management and in-flight services in modular concepts~\cite{uspace_inflight_services_architecture}. These works give a good conceptual basis, but only few of them measure the computing time of planning pipelines that could actually be deployed.

For this paper it is important not to mix up regulatory U-space services and software microservices. In the regulatory sense, a U-space service is a named capability that is offered in a designated airspace volume. A Common Information Services Provider (CISP) publishes common information such as airspace constraints, and a USSP offers services to operators, for example geo-awareness, traffic information and flight authorization~\cite{easa2020uspace}. A software microservice, on the other hand, is simply a unit of software that can be deployed on its own. There is no one-to-one match between the two: one regulatory service may need several software functions, and one software function can support several regulatory services. The planner in this work prepares candidate trajectories and runs a conflict check before the plan is submitted; it is not the authority that approves flights.

\subsection{UAS Path Planning and Conflict Detection}

Corridors are only one of several airspace models proposed for low-altitude urban flights. Other concepts use altitude layers, zones, or mostly unstructured free-flight airspace. The Metropolis project compared these options with regard to capacity at very high traffic densities~\cite{sunil2015metropolis,schneider2014metropolis}, a more recent study compared the performance of different airspace management concepts for UAM~\cite{abdellaoui2025_airspace_uam}, and DLR's UAM air-traffic-management research is reviewed in~\cite{schuchardt2023_atm_uam_review}. If corridors are used, designing the corridor network is a separate research problem from routing single flights through it. Corridor layouts have been derived with multi-commodity network flow and graph search for urban drone delivery~\cite{he2025_air_corridor_planning}, with route-network planning methods for UAM~\cite{li2024_uam_route_network}, and with double-layer structures that separate transshipment from final delivery~\cite{li2025_double_layer_route_network}. In this paper we take the corridor network as given and look at the planning services that use it; the exemplary network of Section~\ref{sec:scenario_setup} is only a benchmark input and not an optimized design. We chose corridors because they fit naturally into a graph that a routing service can work on, because they give simple and predictable routes for early UAM operations, and because several operators plan to start with corridor operations within U-space. The price is less routing flexibility and possibly lower capacity than in less structured airspace.

Search-based planning is still widely used for UAS routing in structured airspace. With corridors, the routing problem becomes a graph problem: nodes are vertiports or route intersections and edges are corridor connections. Classic shortest-path methods and heuristic search such as A* are therefore a good fit for finding the lateral route.

A* works well on corridor networks because a distance-based heuristic guides the search towards the destination and still finds the shortest path~\cite{sahawneh2016_3d_path_planning,a_star_2d3d_comparison_utm}. But graph search alone does not give a complete 4D trajectory. The lateral route still has to be turned into a time-parameterized trajectory with flight phases, climbs and descents, and the performance limits of the aircraft.

The work closest to ours is by Dai et al., who plan conflict-free 4D paths for UAM on a discretized airspace grid with a conflict-aware version of A*. They resolve conflicts inside the search and report computation times~\cite{dai2021_conflict_free_4d_uam}. Our work differs in two ways. Their 4D output is a time-stamped path through grid cells, while we turn a lateral route into a dense trajectory with a flight-management system---and this step turns out to take most of the time. Also, their conflict avoidance is part of the search, while in our case conflict detection is a separate service that is called after the trajectory is generated, so we can measure what this service boundary costs. Surveys of UAS and UAV motion planning cover both kinds of approaches~\cite{ahmad2023_uav_path_planning_survey,zhou2025_uav_motion_planning_survey}, and recent work combines urban path planning with capacity assessment~\cite{feng2025_low_altitude_capacity}, which our single-machine benchmark does not attempt.

Conflict detection and resolution in multi-dimensional trajectory spaces has been studied a lot for trajectory-based operations~\cite{NDMap,enea_porretta_4d_tbo_comparison}. This work describes separation in time-dependent state spaces and is a basis for 4D conflict management. Still, routing, trajectory generation and deconfliction are usually evaluated as separate algorithms and not as parts of one service-based planning pipeline.

\subsection{Microservice Architectures in UAS Applications}

Microservice architectures are popular in distributed software because they are modular, keep concerns apart, and let each part scale on its own~\cite{dragoni2017microservices}. In UAS applications they have helped with maintainability, simulation integration and flexible deployment~\cite{matlekovic2020uav}.

For U-space planning, this style fits well, because topology, route planning, trajectory generation, deconfliction and plan submission can each become their own service. However, there are only few measurements of such planning stacks. In particular, it is not clear which stage takes most of the time when graph routing is combined with 4D trajectory generation.

\textbf{Relation to the surrounding fleet-control architecture.}
The services in this paper are an initial implementation of the planning and deconfliction providers of the fleet-control architecture by Nagel et al.~\cite{nagel2026microservice}. That paper describes the overall microservice structure, the U-plan workflow between fleet operator, USSP and vertiport services, and an integration case study. It does not look inside the planning and deconfliction providers or measure their computing time. This paper fills that part: it describes the planning pipeline (route search, waypoint synthesis, CFMS trajectory generation), defines the NDMap conflict check and how conflicts are mapped to graph edges, and measures latency and conflict resolution for these providers. Everything needed to follow this paper is described here; the citation only gives the wider operational context. The surrounding architecture will be developed further, and its final form may be different from the initial implementation evaluated here.

\noindent
\textbf{Research Gap.}
The literature gives a good theoretical and architectural basis for U-space integration, UAS routing and trajectory-based conflict management. Runtime is reported for single planners, also conflict-aware ones~\cite{dai2021_conflict_free_4d_uam}, but usually as one number for the whole algorithm and not split over the stages that a deployed pipeline runs through. Few works measure the latency of a service-based 4D path-planning pipeline stage by stage, or test conflict checking and re-planning inside the same prototype, where the cost of the service boundary becomes visible. Especially the split between graph search, waypoint synthesis and trajectory generation is rarely measured. In this paper, we address this gap by measuring the planning stages and testing a first NDMap-based lateral re-routing loop on an exemplary vertiport corridor network.
